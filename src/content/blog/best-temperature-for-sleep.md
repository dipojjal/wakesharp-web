It is 2:40 on a December morning, and you are awake for the third time. The thermostat has been at 72 since you came in from the cold, the winter duvet is doing exactly what it was bought to do, and one of your legs is out in the dark, trying to vent the whole system. Your throat feels like paper. Your feet, against all logic, are cold. You get up, turn the heating right down in the mood of someone personally wronged by it, and lie back down.

At 6:30 the alarm goes off in a different climate. The room has done what you asked: it is cold everywhere except under the duvet. You negotiate with yourself until 6:52.

**The short answer:** for most adults, the best temperature for sleep is about 60 to 68°F (16 to 20°C), with your bedding doing the rest. Think cool air, warm bed: your body sheds heat to fall asleep, but the space under the covers should stay snug. In winter, aim for the warmer half of that range, 64 to 68°F (18 to 20°C), especially if you are over 65, pregnant or living with a long-term health condition, and fix cold feet with socks rather than with the thermostat.

## What is the best temperature for sleep?

Most clinical guidance lands in the same band. Cleveland Clinic puts the ideal bedroom at 60 to 67°F (15 to 19°C) and calls anything below 60 too cold ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/what-is-the-ideal-sleeping-temperature-for-my-bedroom)). The Sleep Foundation names about 65°F (18.3°C) as the best room temperature for sleep ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep)). We use 60 to 68°F, the same range as [our post on sleeping through summer heat](/blog/sleep-in-heat-late-summer).

The number hides two things. It assumes ordinary nightwear and bedding: a heavier duvet moves your ideal room temperature down, a single sheet moves it up. And it is an average for healthy adults. Older adults seem to sleep better warmer, and in winter, health guidance raises the floor: the NHS asks for at least 18°C (64°F) in the rooms you use regularly, bedroom included ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/seasonal-health/keep-warm-keep-well/)).

Both halves of that December night were the same mistake: a room set for the wrong hour.

## Why a cool room helps you sleep, and why cold is a different problem

Your core temperature runs on a daily cycle. It starts to fall about two hours before you go to sleep and keeps falling to a low point in the early morning ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep)). Much of that heat leaves through your palms and soles, where extra blood flow warms the skin so the core can cool ([Haghayegh et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31102877/)).

But a sleeping body lives in the bed, not the room. Researchers call the pocket of air between you and the covers the bed climate, and normal sleep goes with a bed climate of about 32 to 34°C (90 to 93°F) at 40 to 60 percent humidity, held fairly steady by bedding even as the room changes ([Okamoto-Mizuno and Mizuno, Journal of Physiological Anthropology, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3427038/)). That is why a 61°F room under a good duvet can feel perfect, and a 72°F room under the same duvet feels like a slow cooker.

The same review holds the finding that matters most in winter. With normal bedding, cooling the room from 17°C to 3°C (63°F to 37°F) did not change sleep stages or how people said they felt. Their hearts noticed anyway: measures of the nervous system's control of heart rhythm shifted. The authors suggest this may partly explain why cardiac events peak in the coldest part of winter, and they advise keeping winter bedrooms above 10°C (50°F) at the very least. The trouble with cold is that it doesn't wake you, so it never prompts you to fix it.

## What is a good temperature to sleep at in the winter?

The same 60 to 68°F, leaning toward the top: 64 to 68°F (18 to 20°C) is where sleep guidance and cold-weather health guidance overlap. The NHS also says it is best to keep bedroom windows closed at night. Winter rarely delivers that number by accident. Heating set for the living room can overheat the bedroom, a winter duvet doubles the load, the air dries out, and your feet, at the far end of the circuit, stay cold. Six fixes, roughly in order of payoff:

### 1. Turn the bedroom down at bedtime, not off

A programmable or smart thermostat can drop the temperature on a schedule. The Department of Energy estimates that turning your thermostat back 7 to 10°F for eight hours a day can save as much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling ([US Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/save/home-upgrades)). For sleep, let the setback stop at the range rather than below it: from a 70°F evening, 65 or 66°F is plenty.

### 2. Layer the bed instead of heating the room

Because the bed climate matters more than the air, two lighter layers beat one heavy duvet: something you can push off at 3 AM without fully waking. If you run hot under a winter duvet in a heated room, change the duvet before you blame the thermostat.

### 3. Put socks on cold feet

Cold feet jam the heat-dumping step that sleep depends on, and people with chronically cold feet may be at higher risk of trouble falling asleep ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/bedroom-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep)). In a small Korean study, six young men slept in a 23°C (73°F) room with and without bed socks. With socks they fell asleep 7.5 minutes faster, slept 32 minutes longer and woke 7.5 fewer times, with no change in core temperature ([Ko and Lee, Journal of Physiological Anthropology, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5921564/)). Six people is tiny, so treat it as a reason to try socks, not a promise. One exception matters: with diabetes or numb feet, wear socks in bed, but never put a hot water bottle or heating pad on your feet ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/preventing-problems/foot-problems)).

### 4. Take the warm bath, then keep the walk back warm

The bath works in December for the same reason it works in August. In the meta-analysis behind it, water at 40 to 42.5°C (104 to 108.5°F), one to two hours before bed and for as little as 10 minutes, shortened the time it took to fall asleep (Haghayegh et al., linked above). The winter catch is the trip back through a cold hallway: chilled skin constricts its blood vessels ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/what-is-the-ideal-sleeping-temperature-for-my-bedroom)), the opposite of what the bath was for. Have a robe and socks waiting.

### 5. Warm the bed before you get in, then switch it off

An electric blanket is best used to take the chill off the sheets, not to heat you all night. In one study, 16 adults were heated through an electric blanket from 2:30 AM until waking. Their core temperature rose 0.18°C, and sleep efficiency between 3:30 and 7:30 fell 5.5 percent, with more shifts between sleep stages and more time awake ([Fletcher et al., Sleep, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10341381/)). Use a timer, or switch it off when you get in. Space heaters need more care, and December to February is when nearly half of home heating fires happen: keep them at least 3 feet from anything that can burn, and turn them off when you go to bed ([NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/home-fire-safety/heating)).

### 6. Treat dry air as its own problem

Dry indoor air can bother the nose and throat, which may be part of that papery 2 AM feeling. Mayo Clinic suggests keeping home humidity between 30 and 50 percent: below that, dry air can also irritate sinuses and cause nosebleeds and cracked lips, and above it, condensation feeds mold, dust mites and bacteria ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/common-cold/in-depth/humidifiers/art-20048021)). A hygrometer tells you which side you are on. If you add a humidifier, fill it with distilled or demineralized water and keep it clean.

## Older adults, and two people who disagree

Older bodies are the exception to "cooler is better." A Boston study followed 50 adults with an average age of 79 for an average of about 200 nights each, using sleep rings and bedroom sensors. Sleep was most efficient and restful at 20 to 25°C (68 to 77°F); compared with 22°C, a 14°C bedroom went with sleep efficiency about 5 percent lower ([Baniassadi et al., Science of the Total Environment, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10529213/)). That is an association in one city, but an experiment points the same way: warming older sleepers' skin by just 0.4°C with a thermosuit almost doubled their deep sleep and cut the chance of waking too early from 0.58 to 0.04 ([Raymann et al., Brain, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18192289/)). The same authors note that older people respond less to a room that is too cold or too warm, so trust a thermometer over a feeling. (Why that deep sleep is worth protecting: [how much deep sleep you need](/blog/how-much-deep-sleep-do-you-need).)

For couples, the argument is real. In indoor-comfort research, women are on average more likely than men to call the same room uncomfortable, especially when it is cool, even though the temperature each calls neutral is usually similar ([Karjalainen, Indoor Air, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21955322/)). The fix is to stop sharing one bed climate. Set the room for the warmer sleeper, give each person their own duvet in a different weight, and let the colder one add socks and a layer. Adding warmth to one side of a bed is easy; taking it out of a whole room is not.

## The cold room at 6:30 AM

Your core temperature is near its low in the early morning, so the alarm finds you at your coldest, in a room that has had all night to cool, beside the warmest place in the house. The cost is not only willpower: in a Japanese survey of about 2,900 adults, morning systolic blood pressure rose 8.2 mm Hg for every 10°C drop in indoor temperature at the survey's average age of 57, and older people and women were more sensitive ([Umishio et al., Hypertension, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31446802/)).

So make the first minute warm on purpose. Schedule the heating to come back on shortly before your alarm, not when you get up. Leave a robe and a pair of socks where your feet land. Then make the first thing you do something that moves you.

## FAQ

### Is 67 degrees good for sleeping?

Yes, for most adults. 67°F (19.4°C) sits inside the 60 to 68°F range most guidance gives, and above the 64°F (18°C) floor UK health guidance sets for bedrooms in cold weather. Older adults may sleep better a little warmer. If you wake up sweating, lighten the bedding before you touch the thermostat.

### Is 72 degrees too hot to sleep?

It is not dangerous, but it is warmer than ideal for most adults, especially under a winter duvet. Some people sleep well at 72°F (22°C) with light bedding. In the Boston study of older adults, predicted sleep time held steady up to about 22°C and fell as rooms got warmer, which makes 72 a sensible ceiling rather than a target.

### Is it better to sleep in a cold room?

Cool, yes; cold, no. Cleveland Clinic calls anything below 60°F too cold, because blood vessels constrict and the cardiovascular system works harder. With good bedding you may sleep straight through a cold room, which is the catch: in a lab study, cold changed heart-rhythm measures without changing sleep stages or how people felt. Keep the room in range, and toward the warm end if you are older or have a heart or lung condition.

### Should you wear socks to bed?

If your feet are cold, yes. Warm feet help heat leave your core, and in a small study, bed socks helped young men fall asleep faster and sleep longer. Skip them on hot summer nights, when feet do better uncovered. If you have diabetes or numb feet, socks are the safe choice; hot water bottles and heating pads on the feet are not.

## Tonight's version

Five minutes, once. Set the bedroom to reach 64 to 68°F at bedtime and to start warming again shortly before your alarm. Swap one heavy duvet for two lighter layers. Put socks under the pillow and a robe on the door.

Then let the morning be easy to walk into: a lit hallway, the kettle, and a room that was ready before you were.
