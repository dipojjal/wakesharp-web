It is 5:48 on a Tuesday in December, twelve minutes before the alarm, and you are awake in the worst possible way. Your heart is going as if you had run for a bus, and there is a knot under your ribs. Before your eyes are properly open, your mind has pulled up the 10 AM meeting, the email you never answered and a vague conviction that something, somewhere, has gone wrong. Nothing has happened yet. The house is dark and quiet. The dread simply got up before you did.

**The short answer:** waking up with anxiety is partly built in, because cortisol rises sharply in the first 30 to 45 minutes after you wake, and the rise grows when you are braced for a hard day (in one study of 196 British civil servants it was nearly three times bigger on a workday than on a weekend day). That surge is alertness chemistry, not a diagnosis. Short sleep, alcohol, big doses of caffeine and an inbox in bed make mornings worse; slow breathing, a plan made the night before, light and movement help. Worry that fills most days for six months or more deserves proper care.

## Waking up with anxiety: what cortisol is doing at 6 AM

The basics are in [our guide to the first 30 minutes after waking](/blog/first-30-minutes-brain-after-waking): cortisol climbs steeply for the first half hour or so, a surge researchers call the cortisol awakening response. What matters for anxiety is what the rise is for, and what sets its size.

A 2025 review in *Endocrine Reviews* proposes two jobs ([Stalder and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39177247/)): mainly, to mobilise resources for the demands your brain expects from the coming day, and secondly, to help the body counter bad emotional experiences from the day before. In a sense, your body reads yesterday's diary and today's calendar, then doses accordingly.

The forecasting starts before you wake. In a 1999 study in *Nature*, volunteers who expected to be woken at a set time showed a marked rise in ACTH, the pituitary hormone that tells the adrenal glands to release cortisol, about an hour before that time ([Born and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/16166)).

The day ahead shows up in the numbers too. Among 196 British civil servants, the rise over the first 30 minutes averaged 10.5 nmol/l on a workday against 3.7 on a weekend day ([Kunz-Ebrecht and colleagues, *Psychoneuroendocrinology*, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14749096/)). In 219 people sampled over six days, those reporting chronic work overload and worry had a stronger rise on weekdays but not at weekends ([Schlotz and colleagues, *Psychosomatic Medicine*, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15039505/)). And in 156 older adults, a day of feeling lonely, sad, threatened or out of control was followed by a bigger rise the next morning ([Adam and colleagues, *PNAS*, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17075058/)).

So the 5:48 dread is not random. Part of it is your body acting on yesterday and gearing up for today before you have had a vote.

## Is morning anxiety caused by cortisol?

Not on its own. The awakening response happens in healthy people every morning, and most of them do not wake up frightened. The link to anxiety is real, but messier than the internet suggests.

A meta-analysis of 147 studies linked a larger rise to job and life stress and a smaller one to fatigue, burnout and exhaustion, while noting inconsistent results ([Chida and Steptoe, *Biological Psychology*, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19022335/)). In 1,427 Dutch adults, a current anxiety disorder came with a higher awakening response, but a modest one, driven mainly by panic disorder with agoraphobia and by people who were also depressed ([Vreeburg and colleagues, *Psychosomatic Medicine*, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20190128/)).

A more useful model: the morning surge supplies the arousal, and your situation and habits of thought supply the meaning. That is why the fixes below work on the meaning and the inputs, not the hormone.

## What makes morning anxiety worse

**A short night.** In a Berkeley sleep lab, a night without sleep raised next-day anxiety by up to 30 percent, and in an online study of 280 people, even modest night-to-night dips in sleep predicted more anxiety the following day ([Ben Simon and colleagues, *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31685950/); [UC Berkeley](https://news.berkeley.edu/2019/11/04/deep-sleep-can-rewire-the-anxious-brain/)).

**Last night's drinks.** Alcohol shortens the time it takes to fall asleep, then disrupts the second half of the night ([Ebrahim and colleagues, *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)), the half that runs into your alarm.

**A lot of caffeine.** In placebo-controlled challenge studies, 400 to 750 mg of caffeine, roughly five cups of coffee, set off panic attacks in about half of people with panic disorder and in under 2 percent of healthy volunteers, and raised anxiety in both groups ([Klevebrant and Frick, *General Hospital Psychiatry*, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34871964/)). Smaller doses are barely studied. Coffee on an empty stomach often gets the blame; I could find no trial that tested it, so treat the dose as the lever.

**An alarm that jolts.** The evidence that a loud alarm harms you is thin, as [our look at alarm sounds](/blog/best-alarm-sound-to-wake-up) explains. But a tone that sends your heart racing every morning is a bad first note, and an easy one to change.

**The inbox in bed.** In a trial with 124 adults, a week of checking email three times a day brought lower daily stress than a week of checking at will ([Kushlev and Dunn, *Computers in Human Behavior*, 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563214005810)). It was not a morning study, but reading the overnight backlog before your feet touch the floor hands the day's demands to a brain already busy forecasting them.

## How to stop morning anxiety: five things that help

### 1. Soften the first minute

Choose an alarm tone that wakes you without making your heart jump, set it as loud as you need and no louder, and test it from the pillow. Then keep email, news and messages closed until you are dressed, ideally with the phone charging [away from the bed](/blog/phone-in-bedroom-at-night). You are not avoiding the day; you are choosing when it starts.

### 2. Breathe out slowly, and call the surge what it is

In a month-long remote trial with 108 people, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing (a slow breath in through the nose, a short second breath to top up, then a long, slow breath out through the mouth) lifted mood more than five minutes of mindfulness meditation and slowed people's breathing ([Balban and colleagues, *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/)). Anxiety fell in every group, meditation included, so the edge was in mood.

While you breathe, name the feeling: my body is getting ready, which is not the same as today going badly. In the lab, people told to treat their arousal during a stressful task as useful had healthier cardiovascular responses ([Jamieson and colleagues, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21942377/)); a dark bedroom is not a lab, but it is a reasonable bet.

### 3. Get up, get into light, get moving

Light and movement will not shrink the cortisol rise (in the studies that first-30-minutes guide describes, both nudged it up); they change what you do with the arousal. Pooling 97 reviews, an umbrella review found physical activity had a medium-sized effect on anxiety symptoms, though most of those reviews were rated critically low in quality ([Singh and colleagues, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36796860/)). Start small: out of bed, lights on or curtains open, a walk to the kettle that turns into a walk round the block.

### 4. Park the worry: tonight on paper, tomorrow in a slot

At bedtime, spend five minutes writing a specific list of what the next day holds. In a sleep-lab study of 57 young adults, a to-do list beat writing about tasks already done for falling asleep quickly, and specific lists worked best ([Scullin and colleagues, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29058942/)).

If worries still arrive at dawn, give them an appointment: in a two-week trial with 53 heavy worriers, a daily 30-minute worry period at a set time and place reduced worry, anxiety and insomnia more than a control approach ([McGowan and Behar, *Behavior Modification*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22977265/)). Preliminary, but simple: note the worry, book it a slot, get up.

### 5. Protect the night and turn down the inputs

Aim for the seven or more hours the [CDC recommends](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html) for most adults; the [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) finds a bedtime from your wake time. If bad mornings follow evenings with drinks, try a dry week. If your first coffee is a big one, try smaller cups for a week and compare.

## When morning anxiety needs more than a routine

A tense week before a deadline is not a disorder, but anxiety disorders are common: an estimated 19.1 percent of US adults had one in the past year ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/statistics/any-anxiety-disorder)). See a doctor or mental health professional if:

- worry has been hard to control on most days for six months or more, alongside three or more of restlessness, tiredness, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and poor sleep, the pattern of generalised anxiety disorder ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/generalized-anxiety-disorder-gad));
- you wake from sleep already in a state of panic. That is nocturnal panic, reported at least once by 44 to 71 percent of people with panic disorder, and cognitive behavioural therapy helps ([Craske and Tsao, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15893248/));
- the anxiety comes with low mood, or is shrinking your life: missed work, cancelled plans, a drink to take the edge off.

There are good treatments, usually a talking therapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), sometimes medication ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/generalised-anxiety-disorder/overview/)). Check the physical side too: a pounding heart that will not stop, or comes with chest pain, breathlessness or fainting, needs emergency care, and palpitations occasionally come from an overactive thyroid or a heart rhythm problem ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-palpitations/)). Low blood sugar, a popular online explanation, is rare without diabetes ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/low-blood-sugar-hypoglycaemia/)); if you take insulin or certain diabetes medicines, raise night-time lows with your care team.

If you are thinking about suicide or harming yourself, or the distress feels like too much to carry, reach out today. In the US, call or text 988, the [988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/), at any hour. In the UK and Ireland, call [Samaritans](https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/contact-samaritan/) on 116 123, day or night. If you are in immediate danger, call 911, 999 or your local emergency number.

## FAQ

### Why do I wake up with anxiety every morning?

Usually it is a normal cortisol surge meeting what your brain expects from the day. The rise runs for 30 to 45 minutes after waking and is bigger on workdays, especially in people who feel overloaded or worry chronically. Short sleep, alcohol and a lot of caffeine add to it; if it happens nearly every day for months, or comes with panic, see a doctor.

### Can lack of sleep cause morning anxiety?

It can make it worse. A sleepless night raised next-day anxiety by up to 30 percent in a Berkeley lab study, and smaller dips predicted more anxiety the following day. It runs both ways, too: disrupted sleep is a recognised feature of every anxiety disorder.

### Why do I wake up with anxiety for no reason?

Often there is a reason, just not a conscious one: the body starts preparing for an expected wake-up before you are awake, and yesterday's stress can enlarge this morning's cortisol rise. Waking from sleep in full panic is different. That is nocturnal panic, and it is worth raising with a doctor.

## The morning after this one

Tonight: five minutes on tomorrow's list, the phone charging out of reach, one drink fewer. Tomorrow: when the surge arrives, name it, breathe out slowly, then get up, lights on, and move before you read a single message. None of this switches the cortisol off, and it is not meant to. The goal is not a calm wake-up. It is deciding for yourself what the first ten minutes are for.
