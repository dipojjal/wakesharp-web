You have seen the sun twice since Sunday, both times through a window.

This morning the alarm went off at 6:30 in a room as dark as 2 AM. You made coffee by the little light over the stove, sat on the edge of the bed, one sock on, for longer than you would admit, and caught the 6:52 train in that blue half-light that has not yet committed to being morning. The sun came up at 7:00 while you were underground. It set at 4:29, halfway through a call about next quarter. And everything now costs more than it should: the alarm, the shower, the reply to the group chat about Saturday that has sat unanswered since the weekend.

It is tempting to file this under willpower, or December, or age. A good part of it is light. Your alarm now rings before the signal your body clock uses to decide that the day has started, and a day spent indoors never quite makes up for it.

**The short answer:** the winter blues are a common, mild dip in mood and energy when the days get short. They are not a diagnosis, and they usually lift on their own. Dark mornings tend to make them worse because you wake up before your body clock has seen any daylight, so the fixes are early light (outdoors within two hours of waking, even on cloudy days), one wake time seven days a week, exercise and keeping your plans. If you feel down for days at a time and stop enjoying things, see a clinician: seasonal affective disorder affects about 5 percent of US adults, and it is treatable.

## Winter blues or seasonal affective disorder?

NIH's Dr. Matthew Rudorfer calls the winter blues "a general term, not a medical diagnosis": fairly common, more mild than serious, and usually gone on its own in a fairly short time ([NIH News in Health](https://newsinhealth.nih.gov/2013/01/beat-winter-blues)).

Seasonal affective disorder is depression that keeps a calendar. Symptoms usually start in late fall or early winter and lift in spring and summer, and a diagnosis needs that seasonal pattern for at least two consecutive years ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder)). About 5 percent of US adults have it, and an episode typically lasts about 40 percent of the year ([American Psychiatric Association](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/seasonal-affective-disorder)). It is more common in women and younger adults, and latitude matters: Rudorfer puts it at about 1 percent of people in Florida and about 10 percent in Alaska.

The winter version has a recognizable shape, which NIMH and Mayo Clinic both compare to hibernating: oversleeping, craving carbohydrates, overeating and weight gain, low energy, and pulling away from people ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder/symptoms-causes/syc-20364651)). The specific cause is still unknown. The leading suspects are a body clock unsettled by short days, and shifts in serotonin and melatonin.

Mayo Clinic's line for seeing a doctor is feeling down for days at a time and being unable to get motivated for things you normally enjoy, especially if your sleep or appetite has changed, you are turning to alcohol for comfort, or you feel hopeless or think about suicide. If you are in the US and having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

## Why is it so hard to wake up in winter?

Light is how your body clock sets itself each day. Light toward the end of your biological night and early in your biological day pulls the clock earlier, while light in the evening pushes it later ([Duffy and Czeisler, Sleep Medicine Clinics, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717723/)). December gets the order wrong. In New York the sun rises at 7:00 on December 1 and at 7:16 on the solstice ([timeanddate](https://www.timeanddate.com/sun/usa/new-york?month=12&year=2026)), so a 6:30 alarm rings in the dark, and a sunset around 4:30 hands the rest of the evening to ceiling lights and screens.

The bigger problem is how little daylight reaches you at all. The clearest numbers come from a small study run near the winter solstice in Colorado ([Stothard et al., Current Biology, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5335920/)). Five adults spent a week on their normal routines and a week camping with no electric light. While awake, they got more than 13 times as much light camping (about 10,300 lux on average) as in their everyday lives (about 750). Living by the sun, their internal night, marked by the hormone melatonin, began about 2.6 hours earlier, and they fell asleep about 2.5 hours earlier.

The detail that matters for your 6:30 alarm: in both settings, melatonin was on average still switched on when people woke up, and it switched off only after they were up and after sunrise. Your body can still be running its night while you are running for the train, which is a plausible reason the first half hour feels like wading.

## How much morning sunlight do you need?

Nobody has set a daily dose for healthy adults. What exists is a floor. In 2022, a group of 18 sleep and light researchers recommended at least 250 melanopic lux at eye level throughout the day (light weighted for the eye cells that set the body clock), met with daylight first wherever possible ([Brown et al., PLOS Biology, 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3001571)). They note that ordinary indoor lighting often falls short of it.

The closest thing to a tested dose for winter low mood comes from a trial in people with SAD who took a one-hour walk outdoors every morning for a week. Half of the walkers remitted, against a quarter of those given low-dose artificial light, and the walks shifted their melatonin rhythm earlier ([Wirz-Justice et al., Journal of Affective Disorders, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8731073/)). It is a single study from 1996, but it suggests ordinary daylight, not only a lamp, can help.

Mayo Clinic's advice points the same way: outdoor light helps even on cold or cloudy days, especially within two hours of getting up, and indoors it helps to open the blinds and sit closer to bright windows ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20364722)). An hour will not fit most workday mornings, so treat it as a target and take what you can get.

Before sunrise, the light has to come from a lamp. A [sunrise alarm clock](/blog/do-sunrise-alarm-clocks-work) brightens the room before you wake, and if your low mood follows the seasons, a [light box](/blog/sad-lamp-light-therapy-guide), usually considered a first-line treatment for SAD, is worth discussing with a clinician.

## How to get rid of the winter blues: a December routine

### 1. Keep one wake time, seven days a week

December invites you to sleep the season off. Mayo Clinic's advice for winter low mood runs the other way: reliable times to wake up and go to bed, and fewer naps and less oversleeping. A long Saturday lie-in also means meeting your morning light later, the opposite of what the clock needs. If you want an earlier wake time, move it in small steps; [becoming a morning person](/blog/become-morning-person-realistic) covers the slow version.

### 2. Make the first ten minutes bright

Turn on the main lights as soon as you are up, not just the bedside lamp, and eat breakfast in the brightest room you have. If you use a light box, this is its slot.

### 3. Get outside within two hours of waking, even when it is grey

Walk part of the commute, take the dog the long way, drink the coffee on the front step, take a call outside. A grey December sky is still daylight.

### 4. Move, outdoors when you can

Exercise is one of the better-supported treatments for depression in general. A 2024 network meta-analysis of 218 trials found moderate reductions in depression from walking or jogging, a real benefit even from light activity such as walking, and a bigger one from vigorous exercise ([Noetel et al., BMJ, 2024](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-075847)). The certainty of the evidence was low, and the trials involved people with depression rather than the winter blues. A lunchtime walk outside buys light and movement in one go.

### 5. Dim the last three hours

The 2022 light recommendations also set an evening ceiling: no more than 10 melanopic lux at eye level from three hours before bedtime. In practice, that means overhead lights off, a low lamp instead, and screens turned down. In December the evening starts at 4:30, which leaves hours of bright indoor light to push your clock later.

### 6. Book the plans before you feel like cancelling them

Pulling away from people is one of the symptoms. The behavioral half of CBT for SAD includes scheduling engaging or enjoyable things into the week, such as lunch with friends, a walk or volunteering ([NIH News in Health](https://newsinhealth.nih.gov/2013/01/beat-winter-blues)). In a trial of 177 adults with SAD, six weeks of this therapy matched light therapy in the first winter after treatment, and two winters later fewer people had a recurrence after CBT (27 percent) than after light therapy (46 percent) ([Rohan et al., American Journal of Psychiatry, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26539881/)). You do not need a diagnosis to borrow the idea: plan Saturday on Tuesday.

## Where WakeSharp fits on a dark morning

Fixes 1 to 3 come down to two moves: get up, and get to the brightest light you can find. [WakeSharp](/download) is an alarm app for iPhone and Android in which each alarm asks you to finish a mission you have chosen, and one mission does exactly that. [First Light](/features/walking-alarm-clock) asks you to walk to a window and hold your phone in the light.

In December, the light at the window often is not daylight yet, and the mission does not pretend otherwise. If the room stays dark, its prompt suggests a window, a lamp or the next room. So on dark mornings, let it walk you to the lamp you were going to sit by anyway, and on weekends or later starts, when there is real daylight, take it to the window.

The honest limits: holding a phone in the light long enough to finish a mission is not a dose of light, and the mission does not reset your body clock or treat low mood. The half hour by the lamp, or the walk after sunrise, is where the light actually comes from. First Light also needs the right permission and supported hardware, so try it once before relying on it. However you quiet the alarm, the morning only counts once the mission is done. Your phone's own controls always work: nothing stops you switching the phone off. And WakeSharp has no sleep tracking and makes no diagnosis, so it cannot tell you whether this is the winter blues or SAD.

## FAQ

### Do we need more sleep in winter?

Possibly a little, but the evidence is thin. Among patients at a Berlin sleep clinic, REM sleep ran about 30 minutes longer in winter than in spring, and total sleep was up to an hour longer than in summer, though that difference was not statistically significant ([Seidler et al., Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9981644/)). In the Colorado camping study, people slept about 2.3 hours longer by natural winter light, mostly by going to bed earlier. None of that is a case for sleeping until 10: the CDC recommends at least 7 hours a night for adults ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)), and for winter-pattern SAD, Mayo Clinic advises cutting back on oversleeping.

### Does vitamin D help with winter blues?

Probably not as a mood fix. In the VITAL trial, 18,353 adults aged 50 and over took vitamin D3 or a placebo for a median of 5.3 years, and vitamin D made no difference to depression risk or mood scores ([Okereke et al., JAMA, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32749491/)). For SAD specifically, NIMH describes the treatment studies as mixed. If you suspect you are low in vitamin D, ask for a blood test; it is a separate question, not a stand-in for morning light.

### What is the January blues?

An informal name for the flat stretch after the holidays, not a diagnosis. NIH News in Health notes that some people slump once the festivities are over, and the mornings stay at their darkest into the new year: in New York, the latest sunrise, 7:20, comes on January 4 ([timeanddate](https://www.timeanddate.com/sun/usa/new-york?month=1&year=2027)). "Blue Monday", the supposed saddest day of the year, began as a travel company's 2005 press release, not a study ([The Naked Scientists](https://www.thenakedscientists.com/articles/interviews/blue-monday-science-fact-or-fiction)). If January feels like more than a slump, the red flags above still apply.

## Tonight, set up tomorrow

Most of this is decided the evening before. Put the lamp where breakfast happens. Set Saturday's alarm to the same time as Tuesday's. Put a 12:30 walk in your calendar, and answer the group chat. Tomorrow, go to the light first and the inbox second.

And if the heaviness lasts for several weeks despite all of that, it is not a willpower problem, and it is not yours to solve alone. NIH's advice is plain: talk to a health care provider. Winter depression is common, and it is generally quite treatable.
