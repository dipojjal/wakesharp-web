The alarm goes off at 3:50 because the airport shuttle comes at 4:20, and you went to bed at 1:30 because packing took longer than packing should. You sit up. You look at the suitcase as though a stranger left it there. When your partner asks if you're okay, you explain, with total conviction, that someone needs to let the dog out.

You do not have a dog.

Then you lie back down. At 4:02 the backup alarm gets you up properly, and you make the shuttle with no memory of the first alarm, the suitcase or the dog. Your partner will be bringing up the dog for years.

That was not ordinary grogginess, and not a character flaw. It was a partial awakening with a predictable recipe: a short night, an alarm landing in deep sleep, and a brain coming back online unevenly.

**The short answer:** sleep drunkenness, or confusional arousal, is a partial awakening, usually out of deep sleep, in which you look awake but are confused, slow and disoriented, often with no memory of it afterwards. Episodes usually last about five minutes but can run to an hour or more. In a survey of 19,136 US adults, 15.2% reported at least one in the previous year, and 84% of them also had a sleep or mental health disorder or took psychotropic medication. Short sleep, sudden awakenings from deep sleep, irregular schedules, sleep apnea and some medications are the usual triggers.

## What is sleep drunkenness (confusional arousal)?

The two names get used interchangeably, though only confusional arousal is an official sleep disorder ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/parasomnias/confusional-arousal)). It belongs to the non-REM "disorders of arousal" with sleepwalking and sleep terrors, all of which begin as an incomplete awakening from deep sleep. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine tells them apart by behaviour: walking points to sleepwalking, screaming and fear to a sleep terror, and staying in bed while confused to a confusional arousal ([AASM Sleep Education, 2026](https://sleepeducation.org/sleepwalking-confusional-arousals-sleep-terrors/)).

The signs are slow speech, confused thinking, poor memory and blunt answers, from someone who appears to be awake. Episodes tend to come out of slow-wave (N3) sleep, which is most common in the first third of the night, though they can happen later or after a daytime nap ([AASM Sleep Education](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/confusional-arousals/)).

Clinicians also use "sleep drunkenness" for a close relative: a markedly exaggerated morning fog that is a core feature of idiopathic hypersomnia ([Trotti, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337178/)). Everyone gets [sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy), the heavy-headed stretch after waking; the difference is degree. Inertia makes you slow. Sleep drunkenness makes you lost: unsure where you are, talking nonsense, and remembering little of it later.

## A brain that is half awake, literally

During these arousals, recordings show sleep-like and wake-like brain activity at the same time: local sleep and local wakefulness side by side ([Castelnovo et al., *Nature Reviews Neurology*, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29959394/)). We described one such recording, made with electrodes inside the brain, in [why you sleep through alarms you don't remember dismissing](/blog/sleep-through-alarm-dismissed-no-memory). Enough of you is awake to discuss the dog. Not enough is awake to notice there isn't one.

One laboratory study shows what tips a brain into that state. Researchers played sounds to ten adult sleepwalkers and ten controls as they slept, both after a normal night and after 25 hours awake. The controls had no episodes. Among the sleepwalkers, 3 in 10 had at least one episode triggered in deep sleep after a normal night, and all 10 did after the sleepless one. The sounds that set off episodes were, on average, about as loud as those that simply woke people ([Pilon, Montplaisir & Zadra, *Neurology*, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18463368/)).

Those were people prone to sleepwalking, which often begins as a confusional arousal, but the recipe is familiar: a sound loud enough to wake you, in deep sleep, after too little sleep. The 3:50 shuttle alarm, in other words.

## How common are confusional arousals in adults?

It depends on how you ask. The AASM puts it at about 3% to 4% of adults and as many as 17% of children. The 15.2% survey figure counts anyone with an episode in the past year; set aside episodes explained by another sleep disorder, a mental health condition or medication, and only 0.9% of the sample had confusional arousal disorder on its own ([Ohayon, Mahowald & Leger, *Neurology*, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25156346/)).

## What causes confusional arousals in adults?

Most lists agree, and the triggers stack:

- **Too little sleep.** Short sleep and recovery from sleep loss are both on the AASM's list.
- **Being woken suddenly.** The AASM lists being forced to wake up, and notes that episodes often start when someone else physically wakes you. Cleveland Clinic adds bright lights, a loud noise and needing the bathroom ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/confusional-arousals)).
- **Irregular hours.** Rotating and night shifts are on the list, and in the Ohayon survey a circadian rhythm sleep disorder, or sleeping nine hours or more, came with a higher risk.
- **Other sleep disorders.** Sleep apnea, periodic limb movements, insomnia and hypersomnia are all on the list, and the AASM expects treating the underlying disorder to make arousals less frequent.
- **Medications.** In the survey, 31.3% of people with episodes took a psychotropic medication, mainly antidepressants, and three prescription sleep drugs carry their own warning (see fix 4).
- **Alcohol.** Drinking before bed is on the AASM's list ([AASM Sleep Education, 2025](https://sleepeducation.org/waking-up-confused/)), though how directly it triggers episodes is still debated: a 2014 letter in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* called the evidence limited but growing and asked for proper studies ([Rumbold, Riha & Morrison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3927444/)).
- **Stress, illness, unfamiliar beds and family history.** Each makes an episode more likely.

## Is sleep drunkenness a sign of a sleep disorder?

Often, yes. In the Ohayon survey, a sleep disorder was present in 70.8% of people reporting episodes and a mental health disorder in 37.4%, with the highest odds in bipolar and panic disorder.

The condition most tied to the name is idiopathic hypersomnia: heavy daytime sleepiness despite a normal amount of good-quality sleep, often with long nights, a real struggle to wake, and naps that last hours without refreshing you ([AASM Sleep Education](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/idiopathic-hypersomnia/)). Bedrich Roth, who proposed "hypersomnia with sleep drunkenness" as a syndrome, described confusion, slowness, poor coordination and a pull back to sleep lasting up to four hours. Across clinical series, 47% of people with idiopathic hypersomnia reported it, and it also turns up in narcolepsy and delayed sleep phase (all from [Trotti](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337178/)).

Where the evidence stops: clinical trials have rarely measured sleep drunkenness, treatment rests on small case series, and the best treatment is not known.

See a sleep specialist if:

- episodes are frequent, someone gets hurt, or there is aggression or another safety concern;
- you are very sleepy in the daytime despite long nights, or naps don't refresh you;
- someone has heard you snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing, all warning signs of [sleep apnea](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/obstructive-sleep-apnea/);
- the confusion regularly runs past the hour mark;
- the episodes began with a new medication.

## How do you stop waking up confused?

One episode after a short night usually needs nothing more than a better night. For a pattern, these are the levers.

### 1. Pay down the sleep debt first

Short sleep is on every trigger list, and in that laboratory study it was the difference between 3 sleepwalkers in 10 having an episode and all 10. Give yourself enough time in bed for two weeks before you judge anything else.

### 2. Put the alarm after your deep sleep, not inside it

Deep sleep is front-loaded (more in [how much deep sleep you actually need](/blog/how-much-deep-sleep-do-you-need)), so an alarm in the first few hours of sleep lands where episodes are likeliest. For an early start, go to bed earlier, so the alarm still falls late in your sleep rather than early in it. Keep your wake time steady seven days a week, and if naps leave you confused, shorten them or drop them.

### 3. Let one sound wake you, at one time

Cleveland Clinic suggests putting your phone on do not disturb or sleep mode so a call or text can't wake you at 1 AM. Your alarm should survive the change: Apple says Do Not Disturb, the Ring/Silent switch and Silent mode don't affect the iPhone's alarm sound ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/118444)), while Android's Do Not Disturb can block or allow alarms, so check that alarms are allowed ([Google](https://support.google.com/android/answer/9069335)). Then test it with your night settings on.

### 4. Take your prescriptions and your nightcap to a clinician

If episodes started or worsened with a new medication, tell the prescriber rather than stopping or changing it yourself. The exception: eszopiclone, zaleplon and zolpidem carry an FDA boxed warning after reports of sleepwalking, sleep driving and other activities done while not fully awake, and anyone who has had such an episode on one should stop taking it and contact their health care professional ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/certain-prescription-insomnia-medicines-new-boxed-warning-due-risk-serious-injuries-caused)). For alcohol, two weeks without a drink in the hours before bed is a cheap experiment.

### 5. Make the first half hour safe

Don't drive, cycle or use machinery until you are clear-headed, and build that buffer into the schedule. Keep the bedside safe: Cleveland Clinic suggests a plastic cup instead of a glass, and the Sleep Foundation suggests clearing space around the bed. And brief whoever shares the room: according to the AASM, trying to wake or console someone mid-episode can make it worse or longer, so keep things calm and let it pass.

### 6. Keep a two-week diary before the appointment

Sleep doctors often ask for two weeks of [sleep diary](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/confusional-arousals/). Log bedtime, wake time, alarms, alcohol, medications and episodes, and ask whoever shares the room to note what they saw, since a witness's description can give the doctor valuable clues ([AASM Sleep Education, 2026](https://sleepeducation.org/sleepwalking-confusional-arousals-sleep-terrors/)). You were, after all, not entirely there.

## FAQ

### Why do I sit up in my sleep?

Sitting up, staring blankly, perhaps saying something odd, then lying back down with no memory of it is the textbook picture of a confusional arousal: a partial awakening out of deep sleep. An occasional episode after a short night or a sudden noise is common and usually harmless. Frequent episodes, injuries or aggression are reasons to see a sleep doctor.

### What are parasomnias?

Parasomnias are sleep disorders that cause abnormal experiences or behaviours as you fall asleep, during sleep or just before you wake ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/12133-parasomnias--disruptive-sleep-disorders)). The non-REM group is confusional arousals, sleepwalking and sleep terrors. The REM group includes nightmare disorder, recurrent isolated sleep paralysis and REM sleep behaviour disorder.

### How long does sleep drunkenness last?

A typical confusional arousal lasts about five minutes, though Cleveland Clinic notes some run to an hour, and the AASM describes episodes of a few minutes up to several hours. In idiopathic hypersomnia, the sleep drunkenness Roth described lasted up to four hours. If your confusion regularly runs past an hour, tell a doctor.

### Is sleep drunkenness dangerous?

The AASM says episodes in adults are usually not dangerous, though rarely they include aggressive behaviour. The everyday risks are practical: a fall, a broken glass, or doing something that needs a clear head before you have one, most seriously driving. Frequent episodes, injury, aggression or heavy daytime sleepiness are reasons to see a sleep specialist.

## The dog, revisited

A single episode is often a brain doing an ordinary thing at a bad moment: leaving deep sleep too abruptly, after too little of it. A pattern deserves a doctor. The rest is unglamorous: more sleep, one alarm time outside the first hours of the night, and nothing else allowed to wake you.

And if someone you live with quotes things you said at 3:50 in the morning, believe them. They were there. You, technically, were not.
