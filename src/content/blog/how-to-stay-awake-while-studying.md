It is 11:40 on a Monday night in December. You are on your bed with a highlighter, a laptop on a pillow and 40 pages of organic chemistry due in your head by 8:30 on Thursday. The lamp is warm and dim, the radiator ticks, and you have read the paragraph on elimination reactions three times without absorbing a word.

Then you blink, and it is 12:26. The highlighter has dragged a yellow comet across page 212, and the coffee you made at nine is cold.

The usual verdict is a character flaw. But look at the setup: horizontal, warm, dim and quiet, with a task that asks nothing back. You did not fail to stay awake. You built an excellent place to fall asleep and then sat in it.

**The short answer:** to stay awake while studying, change the setup that lets sleepiness win: sit at a desk under bright light, study in timed 25-minute sprints that test you instead of rereading, and walk between them. Drink caffeine about 30 minutes before the hardest stretch, so it peaks on time, and finish the last cup about nine hours before bed. If your eyes keep closing anyway, a 10- to 20-minute nap beats another hour of fighting, and the night before the exam, sleep beats studying.

## Why studying makes you so sleepy

Two systems set how alert you feel: sleep pressure, which builds the longer you are awake, and a circadian clock that in young adults tends to run late, so many feel most awake in the evening and come up short on school nights. A review of sleepiness in college students puts numbers on it: half report daytime sleepiness, and 70 percent don't get enough sleep ([Hershner and Chervin, *Nature and Science of Sleep*, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4075951/)).

The same review makes the point that matters at 11:40 PM: sleepiness depends on circumstance. A lecture that asks nothing of you, in a dark, warm hall, can unmask sleepiness that was already there. A bed, a dim lamp and a chapter to reread is that lecture with a pillow.

The task matters too. In a meta-analysis of short-term sleep deprivation, lapses in simple attention showed the largest effect, while reasoning accuracy barely moved ([Lim and Dinges, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438143/)). Rereading leans heavily on that kind of sustained, passive attention. So the fixes below change the room and the task.

## How to stay awake while studying at night: 10 tips

### 1. Get off the bed and turn the lights up

Sit at a desk and make the room bright. Light has an alerting effect of its own: during the early biological night, ordinary room light of about 100 lux produced half the alerting response of 9,100 lux ([Cajochen et al., *Behavioural Brain Research*, 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10996410/)). The catch is melatonin: evening room light delayed its onset in 99 percent of people in one analysis ([Gooley et al., *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism*, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21193540/)). Dim the room for the last hour before bed.

### 2. Study in timed sprints

Decide your breaks before you start. Following 87 university students through a day of self-study, Biwer and colleagues found that those given fixed breaks, such as 6 minutes after every 24 (the Pomodoro technique), reported less fatigue and better concentration and motivation than those who broke when they felt like it, and got as much done ([*British Journal of Educational Psychology*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)). It is one day of data, but "I'll stop when I need to" hands the decision to the most tired part of you.

### 3. Spend every break moving

In a small crossover study of 18 college women chronically short on sleep, 10 minutes of easy-to-moderate stair walking raised feelings of energy more than a 50 mg caffeine capsule or a placebo ([Randolph and O'Connor, *Physiology and Behavior*, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28302573/)). The lift was brief and gave no edge on memory or attention tests, so treat it as a reset, not an upgrade.

### 4. Quiz yourself instead of rereading

When Dunlosky and colleagues graded ten common study techniques, practice testing and spacing study over days rated high utility, while rereading and highlighting, two student favorites, rated low ([*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)). Close the notes, write what you remember, then check. It is better learning, and needing an answer every minute leaves drowsiness less room.

### 5. Drink water, and save cold water for emergencies

In young women, mild dehydration (1.36 percent of body mass) brought more fatigue, lower vigor, poorer self-rated concentration and tasks that felt harder, though most cognitive scores held up ([Armstrong et al., *Journal of Nutrition*, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22190027/)). Keep a bottle on the desk. Cold water on your face is different: in a nap study, face washing after the nap cut sleepiness at once, but the effect was mild and transient ([Hayashi et al., *Clinical Neurophysiology*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14652086/)).

### 6. Time caffeine for the hard part, and stop early

In a classic study, caffeine taken by mouth peaked in the blood about 30 minutes later ([Blanchard and Sawers, *European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*, 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6832208/)), so drink it half an hour before the problem set. The far end matters more: a meta-analysis of 24 studies found an ordinary coffee (107 mg) should be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed to protect total sleep ([Gardiner et al., *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36870101/)). For a midnight bedtime, that is about 3 PM. The [caffeine cutoff calculator](/tools/caffeine-calculator) runs your numbers, and [caffeine's long tail](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-time-sleep) explains why the 9 PM cup still works at 2 AM.

### 7. Nap for 10 to 20 minutes instead of fighting it

After cutting 24 young adults' night to about five hours, Brooks and Lack tested afternoon naps. Ten minutes of sleep brought immediate gains in sleepiness, vigor and cognitive performance, some lasting up to 155 minutes; 20 minutes brought gains that emerged after 35 minutes; 30 minutes began with a spell of grogginess ([*Sleep*, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/)). Those were minutes asleep, so set the [nap timer](/tools/nap-timer) for 20 to leave time to drift off. Nap by early evening, since a late nap spends sleep pressure you need at bedtime; at 11:40 PM, the nap you need is the night.

### 8. Or stack them: the coffee nap

Drink a coffee, then lie down at once for 15 to 20 minutes, so the caffeine arrives as you wake. In a driving simulator, 200 mg of caffeine taken with a nap of under 15 minutes cut incidents on a monotonous afternoon drive to 9 percent of placebo levels, against 34 percent for caffeine alone ([Reyner and Horne, *Psychophysiology*, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9401427/)). Mind the cutoff from tip 6: this one is for early afternoons.

### 9. Chew mint gum

A small effect, but one you can use in a lecture hall. Over an 11-minute test in a darkened lab, chewing gum limited the rise in a pupil-based measure of sleepiness compared with sham chewing or none, though self-rated alertness still fell ([Johnson et al., *Physiology and Behavior*, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22061430/)). The authors suggest mint flavor may help.

### 10. Set an alarm for the end of the night

Most people set one alarm, for the morning. Set another for the evening: the minute you stop studying, worked back from your wake time with enough sleep in between. When it rings, write tomorrow's first task on a sticky note and close the book. A stop time chosen at 6 PM beats one negotiated at 1 AM.

## How to stay awake in class

Class removes most of these levers and adds the passive, dark, warm room the review describes. Afternoons add biology: the post-lunch dip is real, happens even without lunch, and deepens after a high-carbohydrate meal ([Monk, *Clinics in Sports Medicine*, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15892914/)). What still works:

- Sit near the front and the light, and take notes that force decisions: write the question each slide answers, not the slide.
- Chew gum, keep water within reach, and walk at every break.
- Before an afternoon class, eat a lighter lunch and time any caffeine for 30 minutes before the start, inside your cutoff.

One warning. Nodding off after a short night is ordinary; fighting sleep in every class despite 7 or more hours most nights is not. Daytime sleepiness that doesn't improve even after enough sleep is often the most obvious symptom of narcolepsy, which usually starts between ages 7 and 25, sometimes with sudden muscle weakness triggered by laughter ([NINDS](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/narcolepsy)). Loud snoring, breathing that stops and starts, or gasping for air can be signs of sleep apnea ([NHLBI](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea/symptoms)). In surveys Hershner and Chervin cite, 27 percent of students were at risk for at least one sleep disorder, and 16 percent of undergraduates said they had fallen asleep while driving. See a doctor about any of these, and stay off the road while drowsy.

## Is it better to sleep or study the night before an exam?

Sleep, in almost every realistic case. Pooling five decades of studies, Newbury and colleagues found that a night without sleep hurt memory both before learning and after it, with the effect more than twice as large before (Hedges' g 0.62 versus 0.28), though most studies were underpowered and publication bias was likely ([*Psychological Bulletin*, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35238586/)). The night before an exam is both: after the week's learning, and before the morning review you were counting on. The full case, and the least-bad plan if you are behind, is in [the all-nighter math](/blog/all-nighter-vs-sleep-exam).

So the rule that protects exam morning is a stop time. The CDC recommends 7 or more hours of sleep for adults and 8 to 10 for teens ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)). Count back from your alarm, subtract half an hour to wind down, and set tip 10's alarm for that minute.

## The exam-morning plan: two alarms

You protected the night. The remaining risk is the 7:05 version of you, who missed the planning meeting and wants five more minutes. Set two alarms before bed.

The first gets you vertical. [WakeSharp](/download) is an alarm app for iPhone and Android whose alarms ask for a mission when they ring. For an exam morning, pick one that moves you: Walk It Off, one of the [walking missions](/features/walking-alarm-clock), asks for real steps, counted by your phone. Try it once the night before, so the motion permission is granted and your steps register. However you quiet the alarm, the morning only counts once the mission is done.

The second is a backup that shares nothing with the first: a different device, such as an old phone or a plain bedside clock, set ten minutes later. One phone is one battery, one volume setting and one set of permissions, any of which can fail overnight; [tonight's alarm checklist](/blog/alarm-reliability-checklist-tonight) covers what to check.

The limits, plainly. No alarm, ours included, can guarantee you wake up, and your phone's own controls always work: nothing stops you switching the phone off. A mission is there to get you out of bed, not to make you sharper, and WakeSharp has no sleep tracking, so it cannot tell you whether you slept enough. That was settled at your stop time.

## FAQ

### How can I stay awake without caffeine?

Light, movement and short sleep. Room light of about 100 lux gave half the alerting effect of very bright light, ten minutes of stair walking beat 50 mg of caffeine for feelings of energy, and a 10- to 20-minute nap improved alertness for up to two hours. Cold water and mint gum give smaller, briefer lifts.

### How do you remove sleepiness in 1 minute?

You can't remove it, only push it back, because sleep pressure clears only with sleep. In a minute you can stand up, switch on the brightest light, splash cold water on your face or climb a flight of stairs; expect the lift to last minutes. For something longer, a 10-minute nap was the most effective length Brooks and Lack tested.

### Do energy drinks help you study?

They keep you awake, at a price. Energy drinks typically carry 54 to 328 mg of caffeine per 16 ounces, the FDA cites 400 mg a day as an amount not generally linked to negative effects in most healthy adults, and medical experts advise against energy drinks for children and teens ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)). Stimulant pills prescribed to someone else are no shortcut: among 898 undergraduates without ADHD, those who used them showed no GPA gains over their peers ([Arria et al., *Addictive Behaviors*, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27469455/)).

## Before you open the notes tonight

Make three decisions while you are fresh: the minute you stop, the time of your last coffee, and where the backup alarm will sit. Then turn on the big light, sit at the desk and start the first 25 minutes, so that at 8:30 on Thursday you walk in as someone who slept.
