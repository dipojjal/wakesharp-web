It is 11:40 on a Sunday night and your alarm is set for 6:45. A quiz with a cartoon wolf on it has just told you that you are a wolf. Twenty minutes ago a different quiz said bear. You are now on question nine of a third, which wants your email address first, and you have noticed that you are losing sleep to find out when to sleep. Both quizzes were partly right, which is the problem.

**The short answer:** your chronotype is where your body clock sits on the spectrum from early to late. Researchers measure it with the 19-question Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire or with the midpoint of your sleep on days off; across more than 53,000 US time-use diaries, the average weekend midpoint was 3:26 AM. It is a continuous scale, not four boxes: bear, wolf, lion and dolphin come from a popular book and are not formal medical terms. The quiz below takes about two minutes and gives you a rough position, not a diagnosis.

## The chronotype quiz: eight questions, about two minutes

Informal and self-scored: the questions are our own, modelled on what the research questionnaires cover, and they are not validated, so treat the result as a first estimate. Score 1 point for each (a), 2 for (b), 3 for (c) and 4 for (d).

1. **Third morning of a holiday, no alarm set. You wake on your own:** (a) before 6:30, (b) 6:30 to 8, (c) 8 to 9:30, (d) after 9:30.
2. **Same holiday. Your eyes start losing the argument:** (a) before 10 PM, (b) 10 to 11, (c) 11 to 12:30, (d) after 12:30.
3. **Workday, alarm at 7. You could hold a real conversation:** (a) almost at once, (b) within 15 minutes, (c) after half an hour, (d) an hour or more.
4. **Your best stretch for hard thinking lands:** (a) before 9 AM, (b) late morning, (c) afternoon, (d) evening or later.
5. **A friend books dinner for 9:30 PM on a Tuesday:** (a) practically my bedtime, (b) fine, but tomorrow will notice, (c) no problem, (d) finally, a sensible hour.
6. **On days off, compared with workdays, you get up:** (a) about the same time, (b) up to an hour later, (c) one to two hours later, (d) more than two hours later.
7. **How often do you get a second wind after 10 PM?** (a) never, (b) now and then, (c) often, (d) most nights.
8. **The people you live with would call you:** (a) the first one up, (b) fine by 8, (c) best left alone until 9, (d) still up after midnight.

### Your score

- **8 to 14: early type.** The animal scheme would say lion.
- **15 to 24: in the middle,** the most crowded part of the curve. Bear country: 15 to 19 leans early, 20 to 24 leans late.
- **25 to 32: late type.** Wolf territory.

### The number researchers use: your free-day midsleep

Pick a typical day off with no alarm and find the clock time halfway between falling asleep and waking: asleep at 12:30 and awake at 9:00 gives a midsleep of 4:45 AM. In the US diaries, cutoffs at 3:00 and 5:00 AM sorted people into 40 percent early, 44 percent in between and 16 percent late ([Fischer et al., *PLOS One*, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5479630/)).

If you sleep much longer on days off than on workdays, part of that late midpoint is repaid sleep debt, not biology. The Munich method subtracts half the extra sleep, measured against your weekly average: about 40 minutes if your free nights run two hours longer ([Roenneberg et al., *Biology*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6784249/)). If the quiz and your midsleep disagree, believe the midsleep: it is what researchers use.

### The dolphin check

Two yes-or-no questions about how you sleep rather than when: do small noises wake you most nights, and do you often lie awake for long stretches? The animal scheme calls this a dolphin. If the second has been true for months, raise it with a clinician: it can be insomnia rather than a personality, and the first recommended treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), not a new schedule ([American College of Physicians, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136449/)).

## How do I know my chronotype? What the research tools measure

The standard tool since 1976 is Horne and Ostberg's Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ). Its 19 questions cover when you would choose to sleep and work, when you feel best and how alert you are after waking, and score you from 16 (strongly evening) to 86 (strongly morning) ([Kantermann et al., *Journal of Biological Rhythms*, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4580371/)). The original paper checked it against body temperature in 48 people: morning types' temperature peaked significantly earlier in the day ([*International Journal of Chronobiology*, 1976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1027738/)).

The Munich ChronoType Questionnaire, from Till Roenneberg, Anna Wirz-Justice and Martha Merrow, asks what you actually do, separately for workdays and free days, and its headline number is the midsleep you just worked out ([*Journal of Biological Rhythms*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12568247/)).

The gold standard is neither questionnaire but dim light melatonin onset: the evening hour your melatonin starts to rise, measured from samples taken in dim light before your usual bedtime. When Thomas Kantermann's team tested both questionnaires against it in 60 people, each tracked it about equally well, with correlations of about 0.7 ([*Journal of Biological Rhythms*, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26243627/)). Yet people with the same score differed by around four hours in their melatonin timing, so the authors warn against timing light or melatonin treatment from a questionnaire alone. That is the honest ceiling for any quiz, ours included.

## Bear, wolf, lion, dolphin: what the animal types get right and wrong

The animals come from the clinical psychologist Michael Breus, whose book *The Power of When* sorts readers into them with a quiz; Cleveland Clinic dates the framework to the late 2010s ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/chronotype)). Summarising the framework, the Sleep Foundation puts bears at about 55 percent of people and wolves at about 15 percent ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/chronotypes)).

What it gets right: chronotypes exist, the middle of the range is the busiest part, and timing matters. What it gets wrong is the boxes. Cleveland Clinic's sleep specialist notes that the animal labels are not formal medical terminology, and the validated tools place you on a continuous scale; Roenneberg's group calls the colour bands in its own charts [arbitrary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6784249/). Treat its percentages as the framework's estimates. And the dolphin is not a timing type at all, which is why it gets its own check above.

## What to do with your result

### 1. Choose a wake time you can keep seven days a week

A result only matters if it changes when you get up. If your midsleep says late and you control your hours, start later. If you don't, keep the same wake time on days off, within about an hour, the cheapest defence against the [Monday tax of social jet lag](/blog/social-jet-lag-monday-tax). Then let the wake time set the bedtime: the [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) finds it for seven, eight or nine hours, with your own time to fall asleep built in.

### 2. Aim your light

Light moves the body clock either way depending on when it lands: in your biological evening and night it pushes the clock later, and in your biological morning it pulls it earlier ([Khalsa et al., *The Journal of Physiology*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12717008/)). For late types, that means daylight soon after waking and dim evenings. When Kenneth Wright's team took people camping in summer with no electric light, their clocks lined up with sunset and sunrise, and the latest chronotypes moved the most ([*Current Biology*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23910656/)). Early types who wake at 4:30 can run the logic in reverse, with more evening light.

### 3. Put hard thinking where your peak is

In a study of athletes, early and late types peaked at different clock times, peaks tracked how long someone had been awake, and performance swung by up to 26 percent across a day ([Facer-Childs and Brandstaetter, *Current Biology*, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25639241/)). You can rarely move a 9 AM meeting, but you can often move the hard report after it.

### 4. Mind the forbidden hour of sleep

Going to bed at 9:30 tonight to become a lark usually fails, for a reason. In 1986, Lavie described a "forbidden zone" for sleep centred around 8 to 10 PM, when falling asleep was hardest even after a night without sleep ([*Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology*, 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2420557/)). Later work put the zone of lowest sleep tendency roughly one to three hours before habitual bedtime, so it moves with your chronotype ([Strogatz et al., *American Journal of Physiology*, 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3605382/)). Jump your bedtime two hours earlier and you land in it. Move the wake time first, add morning light, and let bedtime follow in small steps.

### 5. Know when it is not a chronotype

A late type who sleeps well when allowed to is not ill, just badly scheduled. But if you regularly can't fall asleep until around 3 AM, can't get up for work or school, and it has lasted three months or more, that may be delayed sleep-wake phase disorder; Mayo Clinic advises seeing a professional when symptoms persist ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/delayed-sleep-phase/symptoms-causes/syc-20353340)). Its mirror image, sleepy by 6 to 9 PM and up for good between 2 and 5 AM with no way to adjust, can be advanced sleep phase disorder, which is more common in older adults ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/circadian-rhythm/advanced-sleep-phase)). Extreme daytime sleepiness deserves a doctor's visit whatever your animal.

## FAQ

### What are the 4 chronotypes?

In Michael Breus's popular framework: the lion (early riser), the bear (follows the sun), the wolf (runs late) and the dolphin (a light, easily woken sleeper). Researchers instead use one continuous scale from early to late, often grouped into morning, intermediate and evening types. The first three animals map roughly onto those groups; the dolphin describes sleep quality, not timing.

### Are sleep chronotypes real?

Yes. Questionnaire scores track the timing of melatonin and body temperature, and a study of 8,753 Finnish twin pairs put about half of the variation between adults down to genes ([Koskenvuo et al., *Journal of Sleep Research*, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17542945/)). Those genes are many and individually small: across 697,828 people, 351 genetic regions were linked to being a morning person, yet the 5 percent with the most morning-leaning variants slept only about 25 minutes earlier than the 5 percent with the fewest ([Jones et al., *Nature Communications*, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30696823/)).

### Can you change your chronotype?

Partly. It shifts with age, later through adolescence to a peak around 19 and earlier after that ([Fischer et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5479630/)), and over months to years it stays fairly stable unless your schedule changes a lot ([Kantermann and Eastman, *Chronobiology International*, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29148844/)). Light and routine can move it: in a randomised trial, night owls who kept earlier fixed sleep and wake times, got morning light and ate at regular times shifted about two hours earlier without losing sleep ([Facer-Childs et al., *Sleep Medicine*, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31202686/)). Our [night owl guide](/blog/night-owl-wake-up-early) turns that into a plan.

### Is the bear chronotype the most common?

In the framework, yes: bears are put at about 55 percent of people. The research version is that chronotypes form a roughly bell-shaped curve, so the middle is the most crowded part. In the US diaries, 44 percent had a weekend midsleep between 3 and 5 AM: the largest group, but not a majority. Move the cutoffs and the percentages move too.

## A starting time, not a personality

Whatever animal you got, the useful output is one clock time: when you can get up seven days a week without a fight. Protect it on Saturdays too, get light soon after it, and give it a few weeks. If mornings still feel like an argument after that, you have not failed at being a bear; you have learned something true about your clock, and the next step is a schedule that fits it, or a clinician if nothing does.
