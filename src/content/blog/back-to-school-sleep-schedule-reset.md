The alarm goes off at 6:15 on the first Monday. Someone who has been reliably falling asleep at 1:30 a.m. and surfacing around eleven is now expected to be dressed, fed, and holding a coherent thought before seven. The alarm gets swatted. The second alarm gets swatted. By the time anyone is actually vertical, breakfast is a granola bar in the car and the first two periods are a write-off. Then it happens again on Tuesday.

If you are the parent in this scene, the temptation is to read it as attitude. If you are the student, the temptation is to read it as a personal failing. Both readings are wrong, and both lead to strategies that do not work. What is actually happening is that a body clock got moved a couple of hours later over ten weeks of summer, and it is being asked to move back in a single night.

Clocks do not work that way. But they do move, reliably and predictably, if you push on the right lever in the right direction. Here is what that takes, and roughly what a week can honestly buy you.

## The gap is a timing problem, not a character problem

Two things drifted over the summer, and they drifted together.

The first is the circadian clock itself: the internal timer that decides when melatonin rises, when core temperature bottoms out, and when your brain is willing to be unconscious. In adolescence, that timer runs genuinely late. This is not a story teenagers invented to justify staying up. Mary Carskadon's group documented the shift toward later sleep timing across puberty as a developmental change in the timing system, [not just a change in social opportunity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17383934/). Later work from the same lineage found that the circadian system in early-to-mid puberty is [more sensitive to evening light](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/100/11/4067/2836092), which pushes the clock later still. The 2018 review by [Crowley, Wolfson, Tarokh and Carskadon](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29908393/) calls the combination of slower sleep-pressure buildup, a delayed clock, and early school bells a "perfect storm," which is a polite way of saying the schedule and the biology are pointed in opposite directions.

The second thing that drifted is habit, and habit is much easier to move than biology. The two are entangled, but only one of them responds to a firmer tone of voice.

This is also why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended in 2014 that middle and high schools [start no earlier than 8:30 a.m.](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/134/3/642/74175/School-Start-Times-for-Adolescents), noting that sleep-wake cycles shift up to two hours later at the start of puberty. Most districts did not take that advice. Which means the reset falls to you, at home, this week.

For reference on what you are aiming at: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's [pediatric consensus statement](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.5866) recommends 9 to 12 hours per night for ages 6 to 12 and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18. The CDC's own figures show the [large majority of high school students](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/healthyschools/sleep.htm) do not hit that during the school year.

## What actually moves a body clock

Light. Overwhelmingly, light, and specifically the timing of it.

Human circadian rhythm shifts in a direction that depends on when the light hits. Khalsa and colleagues mapped this out precisely in a [phase response curve to single bright light pulses](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/jphysiol.2003.040477): light in the hours after your body's temperature minimum (roughly, after your natural wake time) advances the clock earlier, while light in the evening before that minimum delays it later. Same stimulus, opposite effect, decided entirely by the hour.

That is the whole lever. Morning light pulls the clock earlier. Evening light pushes it later. Everything else in a back-to-school reset is logistics around that fact.

The doses are not trivially small, but they are achievable. In a controlled study of adolescents with late bedtimes, [Misiunaite, Eastman and Crowley](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.00099/full) found that two mornings of roughly two and a half hours of bright light produced about a one-hour phase advance, while shorter exposure did not clearly beat the room-light control. Real outdoor morning light is far more intense than anything indoors, even under cloud cover, which is why "eat breakfast by the window" is weaker than "eat breakfast on the porch" and much weaker than "walk the dog."

One more piece of physics matters for the plan below. You cannot force sleep onset. Telling a delayed teenager to go to bed at 9:30 produces ninety minutes of lying in the dark being annoyed. You *can* control wake time. Pull wake time earlier, get light into the eyes at that new hour, and sleep pressure plus the shifted clock will drag bedtime along behind it. Advance the wake end; let the bedtime end follow.

## The seven-day reset

Set your target first. Count backward from the bus or the first bell: leaving time, minus getting-ready time, equals target wake time. Then subtract the age-appropriate sleep need to find the target bedtime. Write both numbers down, because vagueness is what kills this.

Then be honest about the size of the gap. Under two hours is comfortably closeable in a week. Three to four hours is not, and pretending otherwise sets everyone up to feel like they failed. Close what you can this week, keep going into the first two school weeks, and expect the first few school mornings to be genuinely hard rather than magically fine.

**Day 1 (today).** Do nothing to bedtime. Wake up 30 minutes earlier than the summer average, and get outside within about 20 minutes of waking, for 20 to 30 minutes. Breakfast outside, a walk, a bike loop, sitting on the steps with a phone. The activity does not matter; the eyes being open under daylight does. Expect the first hour to feel awful. That thick-headed, cannot-form-a-sentence stretch is [sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy), it is normal, and it fades within 15 to 60 minutes as long as you do not go back to bed.

**Day 2.** Wake 15 minutes earlier than Day 1. Same morning light. Tonight, start the evening side: dim the house lights after dinner and move screens off the face and onto a desk or table, which cuts the light reaching the eyes without requiring anyone to surrender a device.

**Day 3.** Another 15 minutes earlier. Move dinner earlier if it has drifted late. Set a "wind-down alarm" 45 minutes before target bedtime, which is a cue, not a curfew.

**Day 4.** Another 15 minutes. This is usually the worst day: enough accumulated sleep debt to feel rotten, not enough clock shift to feel any benefit. Ride it out. Naps, if truly necessary, should be under 30 minutes and before 3 p.m., because a 5 p.m. nap steals the sleep pressure you need at 10 p.m.

**Day 5.** Another 15 minutes. By now bedtime should be sliding earlier on its own. If it is not, do not add pressure at the bedtime end. Add light at the morning end instead.

**Day 6.** Another 15 minutes, ideally landing at or near the target wake time. Do a full dress rehearsal: same wake time, same clothes-and-bag routine, same breakfast, same departure time. Find out now that the bathroom queue takes twelve minutes longer than anyone thought.

**Day 7.** Hold. Do not push further. Same wake time, same light, and get bags packed the night before so that morning decisions are already made.

Across seven days that is roughly a 90-minute to two-hour advance in wake time, with bedtime trailing behind it. That is a real, achievable shift, and it is about the ceiling for one week without light exposure that most families cannot realistically arrange.

## The weekend is where resets die

The single most reliable way to undo everything above is sleeping until noon on Saturday.

Crowley and Carskadon tested this directly and found that a typical late weekend "recovery" sleep schedule [delayed circadian phase](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20795887/) in older adolescents, so Monday starts from further behind than Friday did. Till Roenneberg's team named the general pattern [social jet lag](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17936039/): the standing mismatch between the body clock and the social clock, re-inflicted every weekend.

The workable compromise is not "no sleeping in." It is a cap. Allow at most one extra hour on weekend mornings, and get light shortly after waking. An hour of extra sleep on Saturday costs you little. Four hours costs you the week.

For older students running their own schedule, the other weekend hazard is the 11 p.m. to 1 a.m. stretch that feels like the only unclaimed time in the day. That is [revenge bedtime procrastination](/blog/revenge-bedtime-procrastination), and it is worth solving by moving personal time earlier rather than by trying to want it less.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to reset a sleep schedule for school?

Plan on one to two weeks for a shift of one to two hours, and longer for anything bigger. The practical constraint is that a body clock shifts by a limited amount per day, and morning light is what determines how much. A week of 15-minute daily advances plus real outdoor morning light typically buys 90 minutes to two hours. If the gap is three or four hours, start earlier if you can, and otherwise plan to finish the reset during the first two weeks of school rather than before it.

### Should I move bedtime or wake time first?

Wake time. You cannot make anyone fall asleep on command, but you can control when they get up and when light hits their eyes, and those two together pull bedtime earlier on their own. Advancing bedtime first mostly produces frustrated lying awake, which teaches the brain to associate bed with being annoyed. Move the morning; let the evening follow.

### Is it okay to let my teenager sleep in on weekends during the school year?

An extra hour, yes. A whole morning, no. Crowley and Carskadon's work shows that late weekend recovery sleep [pushes circadian phase later](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20795887/), which makes Monday harder than Friday was. Capping the weekend lie-in at about an hour and getting daylight soon after waking keeps the clock roughly where you put it. If your teen is so wrecked by Saturday that they need four extra hours, the school-week schedule itself is short on sleep, and that is the thing to fix.

### What if my kid falls asleep fine but cannot wake up?

Rule out the boring explanations first: total sleep time that is genuinely too short, a bedroom that stays dark long after the alarm, and an alarm that is easy to silence without waking. Then look at how the morning is structured, since getting light and movement in quickly shortens the grogginess window considerably. Persistent, severe difficulty waking despite adequate sleep opportunity, or daytime sleepiness that does not respond to a fixed schedule, is worth raising with a pediatrician rather than solving with a louder alarm.
