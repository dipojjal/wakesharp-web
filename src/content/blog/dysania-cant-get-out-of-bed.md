It is 6:52 on a Thursday in December. The alarm went off at 6:30, you turned it off, and you have been awake ever since, lying under the duvet with the phone face down on your chest, doing arithmetic. Skip the shower and you can stay until 7:10. At 7:10 you renegotiate. The room is dark, and getting up feels less like a choice than a weight you would have to lift with your whole body.

At 7:34 you finally swing your legs out, late and faintly ashamed. The strange part is that ten minutes later, dressed and holding a coffee, you feel more or less fine.

If that is most of your mornings, the internet has a word for it: dysania. Useful, as long as you don't mistake it for an answer.

**The short answer:** dysania is an informal name, not a medical diagnosis, for finding it extremely hard to get out of bed. The ordinary explanations are too little or badly timed sleep and sleep inertia, the grogginess that usually eases within about 30 minutes of waking. If it happens most days for two weeks or more, or comes with low mood, loud snoring or exhaustion that rest doesn't fix, see a clinician: depression, sleep apnea, chronic fatigue and medication side effects can all sit behind it.

## What is dysania?

The Sleep Foundation defines dysania as extreme difficulty getting up from bed, or feeling unable to leave it at all ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/excessive-sleepiness/dysania)). It is not a diagnosis: the same page notes that the medical community doesn't widely recognize the term, that people use it inconsistently, and that it is better read as a possible symptom of something else.

So dysania names the experience, not the cause. "I have dysania" explains nothing. "I have found it extremely hard to get up most days for a month" is the start of a useful conversation with a doctor. It is not laziness either: lying awake dreading the cold floor is not a moral failing.

## Why getting out of bed can feel impossible

### Too little sleep, and the grogginess that follows

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend 7 or more hours a night for adults ([Watson et al., Sleep, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4434546/)). Fall short and you wake into heavier sleep inertia, the temporary sleepiness, disorientation and sluggish thinking that follow waking. Performance usually returns to its pre-sleep level within about 30 minutes, though full recovery can take an hour or more, and inertia is worse after prior sleep loss and when you wake near the low point of your body clock ([Hilditch and McHill, Nature and Science of Sleep, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)).

So the decision to get up is made exactly when you are least equipped to make it ([more on sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy)).

### A body clock that runs late

If 10 AM on a Saturday is easy and 6:45 on a Tuesday feels brutal, your clock may run late. In delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, sleep sits two or more hours later than social norms: getting up for work or school is hard, yet on their own schedule people with it usually sleep a normal amount, just later. The AASM estimates that 7 to 16 percent of teens and young adults may have it ([AASM Sleep Education](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/delayed-sleep-wake-phase/)).

### Sleep that doesn't refresh you

Sleep apnea breaks the night into pieces: breathing stops and starts, often with loud snoring and gasping or choking sounds, and the result is heavy daytime tiredness and sometimes a headache on waking ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sleep-apnoea/)). Hypersomnia runs the other way: feeling extremely sleepy despite getting enough sleep, sometimes sleeping 11 hours or more and still struggling to wake ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21591-hypersomnia)).

### Exhaustion that rest does not fix

In myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), fatigue lasts six months or longer and isn't relieved by sleep or rest, sleep is unrefreshing, and symptoms get worse after physical or mental effort that was no problem before the illness ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/signs-symptoms/index.html)). That last feature matters for the advice below: pushing through can backfire.

### Medicines and other medical causes

Sleeping pills, tranquilizers, antihistamines, certain painkillers and some psychiatric medicines can all make you drowsier than normal, and so can an underactive thyroid ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003208.htm)). Iron deficiency anemia can also leave you short of energy ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tiredness-and-fatigue/)). If a medicine might be involved, raise it with the prescriber rather than stopping or changing it yourself.

## Is dysania a sign of depression?

It can be, though on its own it proves nothing. Fatigue or feeling slowed down, sleeping too much or too little, and losing interest in things you used to enjoy all appear on the National Institute of Mental Health's list of depression symptoms. A diagnosis requires symptoms most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression)).

Sleep and mental health pull on each other in both directions. In one review, about three quarters of people with depression had insomnia symptoms, around 40 percent of young adults with depression slept too much, and insomnia in people who were not depressed was a risk factor for depression later ([Nutt, Wilson and Paterson, Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience, 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181883/)).

One question can help, though it cannot diagnose anything: does the heaviness lift within an hour of getting up, as sleep inertia does, or does a flat, empty feeling stay with you most of the day? The second deserves a conversation with a doctor or therapist. Depression is a medical condition, not a failure of willpower, and it is treatable, usually with talk therapy, medication or both. A clinician can also check for thyroid disorders and medicines that cause the same symptoms (NIMH).

If you are having thoughts of death or suicide, don't wait for a better morning: in the US, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, or call 911 if your life is in danger. Elsewhere, call your local emergency number.

## When to see a clinician

See a doctor if hard mornings come with any of these:

- They have happened most days for two weeks or longer, or they are getting worse.
- Low mood, emptiness or loss of interest comes with them.
- You have thoughts of death or suicide: call or text 988 today rather than waiting for an appointment.
- Someone has heard you snore loudly, stop breathing, gasp or choke in your sleep, or you often wake with a headache.
- You sleep 11 hours or more and still feel very sleepy, or you fall asleep without meaning to.
- You have felt tired for a few weeks without knowing why, or alongside weight loss or mood changes ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tiredness-and-fatigue/)).
- Rest does not relieve the exhaustion, and effort makes it worse.
- It began around the time you started a new medicine.

In the meantime, don't drive while you feel drowsy.

## How do I make myself get out of bed?

The aim is not to win an argument with yourself at 6:45 but to arrange things so it barely starts. These help most with inertia, habit and dark mornings; they don't treat the causes above.

### 1. Decide the first two minutes tonight

Before you sleep, choose one small, physical first step: sit up, feet on the floor, walk to the kettle. Therapists treat depression with a structured version of this act-first idea, called behavioral activation: scheduling specific, rewarding activities rather than waiting to feel like doing them. A meta-analysis of 26 randomized trials with 1,524 participants found it beat control conditions, though most trials were low quality with short follow-up ([Ekers et al., PLOS ONE, 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0100100)). The morning version borrows only the order: action first, motivation second.

### 2. Put the alarm where you have to stand to reach it

Across the room, by the door. No trial we know of has tested this, but by the time the alarm is quiet you are vertical, which removes the hardest step. Clear the path and keep a lamp within reach, especially near stairs.

### 3. Let light in as you sit up

Regular light and darkness is the main signal that keeps your body clock in step with the solar day, light early in your biological morning shifts the clock earlier, and brighter light raises alertness ([Duffy and Czeisler, Sleep Medicine Clinics, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717723/)). Open the curtains or switch on the brightest lamp the moment you sit up; if it is still pitch dark, [dark winter mornings](/blog/winter-blues-dark-mornings) need their own plan.

### 4. Fix the sleep that feeds it

Aim for 7 or more hours and get up at the same time every day ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)); the Sleep Foundation's advice for dysania adds weekends too. If you move the alarm earlier, move bedtime with it. If you feel flat all day, not only at 6:45, [why you might always be tired](/blog/why-am-i-always-tired) covers the longer list.

### 5. Keep the bed for sleep

The Sleep Foundation also suggests keeping the bed for sleep and sex, not work or meals. When the bed is also the office and the dining table, leaving it means leaving everything. Move the laptop and the late-night scrolling to a chair.

### 6. Shrink the target on bad days, and know when not to push

Some mornings the realistic win is sitting up, or making it to the sofa, and that counts. If effort reliably leaves you worse afterward, stop pushing and see a doctor (the ME/CFS note above). And if every morning is a bad one, you have reached the two-week flag.

[WakeSharp](/download) is an alarm app that asks for a mission when it rings. Some missions put the first task of the day somewhere other than the bed:

- **Walk It Off** asks for real steps, counted by your phone. One of the [walking missions](/features/walking-alarm-clock), it needs the motion permission. Walk carefully, especially in the dark.
- **Photo Proof** asks you to retake the photo of the spot you chose the night before. Choose the kettle, with a lamp you switch on each morning, and the first job of the day is over there, not under the duvet ([photo missions](/features/photo-alarm-clock)).
- **Fetch** asks you to go and find something, such as something blue or something you drink from.

However you quiet the alarm, the morning only counts once the mission is done. That is fix 1 built into the alarm: the first task is decided before you sleep, not negotiated at 6:45.

The limits, plainly: nothing in the app checks that you have left the bed, and some missions can be finished sitting up. WakeSharp does not measure sleep, and no alarm guarantees you wake up. Your phone's own controls always work: nothing stops you switching the phone off. And if depression or illness keeps you in bed, a more demanding alarm is the wrong tool; save missions for mornings that are about inertia and habit.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between dysania and clinomania?

Dysania is difficulty getting up: you may want to rise and find you can't. Clinomania is the desire to stay: a strong, sometimes obsessive pull toward the bed. The Sleep Foundation treats them as closely linked, and neither is a formal diagnosis. Either way, the useful question is what is behind it.

### Why do I have difficulty waking up?

Usually too little sleep, an irregular schedule or an alarm that lands in your biological night, all of which make sleep inertia heavier. A late-running body clock, sleep apnea, hypersomnia, depression and some medicines can do it too. If you sleep 7 or more hours at regular times and still struggle most mornings for weeks, see a doctor.

### Is there a test for dysania?

No. Dysania is not a diagnosis, so there is no validated test, and an online quiz can only confirm that mornings are hard. A clinician looks for what sits behind it: an exam, questions and lab tests for problems such as thyroid disorders or anemia, and sleep testing if apnea is suspected. A week or two of sleep diary (bedtimes, wake times, how you felt) makes the appointment more useful.

### What is oversleeping?

Oversleeping means regularly sleeping well past what your body needs. The AASM and Sleep Research Society recommend 7 or more hours for adults and say more than 9 can suit young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people who are ill; for others, whether it carries health risk is uncertain (Watson et al.). Sleeping 11 hours or more and still feeling very sleepy is a sign of hypersomnia worth taking to a doctor (Cleveland Clinic). Dysania is a different complaint: getting up can feel impossible after six hours or after ten.

## Tomorrow at 6:30

Tonight, decide the first two minutes and put the phone across the room. Tomorrow: sit up, feet down, light on, walk to whatever you chose. If that works, the problem was mostly inertia, habit and darkness, and those respond to arrangement.

If it doesn't, and it hasn't for weeks, that is not a character flaw. It is information, and the right next step is an appointment, not a stricter alarm.
