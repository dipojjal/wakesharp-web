It is 11:40 PM. You chewed a raspberry gummy at 10:30 because the label said 10 mg, and 10 mg sounded like it meant business. Seventy minutes later you are awake, staring at the ceiling, doing the arithmetic on how many hours are left. Tomorrow you will surface at 6:30 feeling like you were dredged up from a lake, and you will draw one of two conclusions: melatonin does not work on you, or you need 15 mg.

Both conclusions are wrong, and they are wrong in the same direction.

## Melatonin is a "when", not a "how much"

The mental model most people carry is that melatonin is a mild sleeping pill: more milligrams, more sleep. That model is the entire problem.

Melatonin is a hormone your brain releases in response to darkness, and, as the [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health puts it](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know), it helps with the timing of your circadian rhythms. It is closer to a memo than a sedative. It does not switch off your cortex. It tells the body's clock what time it thinks it is outside, and the clock adjusts its schedule accordingly over the following days.

That distinction predicts everything else. A memo delivered at the right moment changes the schedule. The same memo delivered five times louder at the wrong moment just makes noise, and the noise lingers into the morning.

The averaged effect on plain sleep onset is genuinely small. A [2013 meta-analysis in PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0063773) pooled 19 trials and found melatonin shortened time to fall asleep by roughly 7 minutes and added roughly 8 minutes of total sleep versus placebo. Real, repeatable, and not the transformation the packaging implies. If you were expecting a knockout, seven minutes is going to feel like nothing.

## Why more milligrams do not buy more sleep

The dose-response work here is old and unusually clear. Researchers in Richard Wurtman's group at MIT tested melatonin against placebo in older adults with insomnia, comparing 0.1 mg, 0.3 mg and 3.0 mg taken 30 minutes before bed. The results, [published in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism in 2001](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/86/10/4727/2849013), found the 0.3 mg physiological dose restored sleep efficiency while bringing blood melatonin into the range a healthy young adult produces naturally.

The 3.0 mg dose was the instructive one. It left plasma melatonin still elevated into the daylight hours, and it induced hypothermia. That is the mechanism behind the gummy hangover: you did not take a sleeping pill that lasted too long, you sent a darkness memo that was still being read at 9 AM. Your body clock spent the morning being told it was the middle of the night. If you have ever wondered why the fog after a big dose feels different from ordinary grogginess, that is why, and it stacks on top of the [normal sleep inertia everyone gets on waking](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy).

Now consider that a 10 mg gummy is more than thirty times the physiological dose, and that some products stack two per serving.

## Timing beats dose, and the timing is earlier than you think

If melatonin is a clock signal, the question stops being how much and becomes how many hours before what.

Circadian researchers map this with a phase response curve: the same dose shifts your clock earlier or later depending on when you take it relative to your own dim light melatonin onset, the point in the evening when your body starts producing melatonin on its own. Helen Burgess and colleagues built these curves for two doses and [published them in 2010](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/95/7/3325/2596406). The largest advances from 0.5 mg came when it was taken in the afternoon or early evening, roughly 2 to 4 hours before that natural onset. When each dose was given at its own optimal time, 0.5 mg and 3.0 mg produced shifts of similar size.

Read that last sentence twice. Six times the dose bought no extra shift. What it bought was a longer tail.

In practice, natural melatonin onset lands roughly 2 to 3 hours before your habitual sleep time, which puts the useful window for a phase shift several hours before bed, not at bedtime with the lights already off. Clinical guidance reflects this: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's [guideline on circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders](https://aasm.org/resources/clinicalguidelines/crswd-intrinsic.pdf) suggests strategically timed melatonin for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, in small doses, taken in the early evening rather than at lights out.

## The label may not be the dose

American melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, which means the [FDA does not approve it for safety or review its labelling before it goes on the shelf](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements). Manufacturers are responsible for that themselves.

Two content analyses show what that produces. Erland and Saxena [tested 31 products in 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855744/) and found actual melatonin content ranging from 83% below to 478% above the labelled amount, with more than 71% of products missing their label claim by more than 10%, and lot-to-lot variation within a single product as wide as 465%. Some samples also contained serotonin. In 2023, Pieter Cohen and colleagues [analysed 25 melatonin gummy products in JAMA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37097362/): 22 of 25 were inaccurately labelled, actual content ran from 74% to 347% of the declared amount, and one product contained no detectable melatonin at all.

So the person taking 10 mg to be safe may be taking 3 mg or 34 mg, and will not find out which. Smaller doses are not only better matched to the biology, they are also less exposed to this problem: a 400% overshoot on 0.5 mg is a different event from a 400% overshoot on 10 mg.

## What to do instead

1. **Start absurdly low.** In the range of 0.3 to 1 mg. If your bottle only sells 5 mg or 10 mg tablets, buy a smaller size rather than trying to quarter a gummy. Going up is a decision to make with a clinician, not a reflex after one bad night.
2. **Move it earlier before you move it higher.** If you are trying to shift a late body clock, evening timing several hours before your target bedtime does more than adding milligrams at 11 PM. If you are simply hoping to feel drowsy, see point 5.
3. **Treat it as a several-night nudge, not a nightly pill.** Clock shifts accumulate across days. One dose is a memo; a week of consistently timed doses is a policy change.
4. **Keep the dark part of the deal.** Taking a darkness hormone while a bright screen argues the opposite case is a coin flip. Dim the room for the last hour.
5. **Audit the obvious saboteurs first.** [Caffeine has a long tail](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-time-sleep), alcohol fragments the second half of the night, and a bedtime that drifts by two hours makes any timing signal meaningless. It is also worth [working backward from your wake time to a realistic bedtime](/blog/what-time-to-go-to-bed-work-backward) before you conclude that a supplement is the missing piece.

## Where melatonin genuinely earns its keep

The evidence is not uniformly thin. It is thin in one specific place and reasonable in others.

For **delayed sleep-wake phase disorder**, the body clock that naturally wants 3 AM to 11 AM, strategically timed low-dose melatonin has positive clinical endorsement, as above. For **jet lag**, the [Cochrane review by Herxheimer and Petrie](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD001520_melatonin-prevention-and-treatment-jet-lag) found it effective for flights crossing five or more time zones, at 0.5 to 5 mg taken near bedtime at the destination, and specifically warned that taking it earlier in the local day can make things worse. Some shift-work patterns are worth a conversation with a clinician for the same reason: they are timing problems, and melatonin is a timing tool.

For plain **chronic insomnia in adults**, the AASM's [pharmacologic treatment guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27998379/) suggests clinicians not use melatonin for sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia. That is a weak recommendation reflecting weak evidence, not a ban, but it should temper expectations considerably.

And melatonin does approximately nothing for a racing mind at midnight, for untreated sleep apnea, for the alcohol you drank at 9 PM, for pain, or for [the hour of scrolling you take as compensation for a day that belonged to other people](/blog/revenge-bedtime-procrastination). None of those are clock problems. A clock signal will not touch them.

## The part an alarm can actually hold

Every melatonin protocol above assumes a fixed reference point: a wake time that does not move. Shift the morning around and the evening signal has nothing to anchor to.

That is the honest place WakeSharp fits. A mission alarm asks for something that needs a working brain, arithmetic in Mind Games or retaking the photo of the spot you chose the night before with Photo Proof, so the wake time you chose is the wake time you actually keep. However you quiet the alarm, the morning only counts once the mission is done. The limit matters too: your phone's own controls always work (nothing stops you switching the phone off), no app can guarantee that anyone wakes up, and none of this shifts your circadian phase by itself. ## Talk to a clinician, genuinely

This is general information, not medical advice, and melatonin is a hormone rather than a vitamin.

Bring it up with a clinician or pharmacist before starting, and definitely if any of these apply. **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** research on safety here is lacking, so this is a conversation, not a self-serve decision. **Anticoagulants and antiplatelet medication, and epilepsy:** NCCIH advises medical supervision for both. **Children:** the AASM issued a [health advisory on melatonin use in children and adolescents](https://aasm.org/advocacy/position-statements/melatonin-use-in-children-and-adolescents-health-advisory/) urging parents to speak with a pediatric professional first and to store it like any other medicine. The reason is in the CDC's data: [pediatric melatonin ingestions reported to US poison centers rose 530% between 2012 and 2021](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/71/wr/mm7122a1.htm), five children required mechanical ventilation, and two died. Candy-shaped, unregulated, and left on the nightstand is a bad combination.

And if you have loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, or daytime sleepiness that no amount of sleep fixes, no supplement is the answer to that. Get it looked at.

## FAQ

### How much melatonin should I take?

Lower than the bottle suggests. The dose-response research points to roughly 0.3 to 1 mg as the physiological range, and the 2001 MIT-group trial found 0.3 mg restored sleep efficiency while 3.0 mg left melatonin elevated into the next morning. The common 5 mg and 10 mg products are well above anything the evidence supports as more effective. Confirm a dose with a clinician, particularly if you take other medication.

### When should I take melatonin?

For a plain wind-down, 30 to 60 minutes before bed is the usual instruction. For actually shifting a late body clock earlier, phase response curve research suggests low-dose melatonin works best taken hours earlier, in the late afternoon or early evening, roughly 2 to 4 hours before your natural melatonin onset. For jet lag, the Cochrane evidence favours bedtime at your destination, and warns against taking it earlier in the local day.

### Why does melatonin make me groggy in the morning?

Usually because the dose was large enough to keep circulating past sunrise. High doses have been shown to leave plasma melatonin elevated into daylight hours, which reads to your body as an extended night. Cutting the dose rather than the habit is the first thing to try. Note also that label accuracy is poor, so your 5 mg may have been considerably more.

### Is it safe to take melatonin every night?

Short-term use appears safe for most adults, but long-term safety data is limited, per NCCIH. Nightly indefinite use is worth a conversation with a clinician rather than a default, especially in children, during pregnancy or breastfeeding, alongside blood thinners, or with epilepsy. If you have needed it every night for months, the underlying question is what the melatonin is compensating for.
