It is 1:35 on Monday morning, and you have been in bed since 11:15 doing everything the articles say. Phone face down across the room. Bedroom cool and dark. Your legs are tired, your head is wide awake, and some part of your brain has chosen this moment to replay a meeting from 2019.

The alarm is set for 6:40. You will get up in the dark, stand in the kitchen under every light you own, and start feeling like a person at about 10:30. By 9 tonight you will be sharp and productive, and then it will be 1:35 again.

You have probably been told this is a discipline problem. Mostly it is a timing problem: your body clock runs late, and your calendar runs early.

**The short answer:** a night owl is someone with a late chronotype, a body clock that runs later than average, so sleepiness arrives late and early alarms land in what is still your biological night. It is about half genetic, according to twin studies, and age and light matter too. You probably can't turn a strong owl into a lark, but light and regular timing can move the clock an hour or two: in a three-week trial, night owls moved their sleep and their body clocks about two hours earlier.

## What is a night owl?

Chronotype is the timing your body prefers for sleep, usually measured as the midpoint of sleep on days off, when no alarm is involved. In American time-use diaries from 53,689 people, the weekend midpoint averaged about 3:26 AM, and the earliest and latest sleepers were almost ten hours apart ([Fischer et al., PLOS One, 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0178782)). By one common cut-off, a midpoint after 5 AM makes you a late type, as 16 percent of the sample were. Night owl vs early bird is not two species. It is a bell curve, and owls live on its late side.

Left to run without light cues, the internal day runs a little longer or shorter than 24 hours depending on the person, and people whose day runs long tend to be night owls ([Wright et al., Journal of Biological Rhythms, 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2714089/)). Light resets the clock daily, in a direction set by timing: light after your body's overnight temperature low pulls the clock earlier, and light before it, in the evening and early night, pushes it later ([Khalsa et al., The Journal of Physiology, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12717008/)). Sleep through the morning, sit up under bright lights at midnight, and you get the delay without the correction.

## Is being a night owl genetic?

Partly. In 8,753 pairs of Finnish adult twins, genes accounted for about half of the variation in whether people leaned morning or evening ([Koskenvuo et al., Journal of Sleep Research, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17542945/)). A genome-wide study of 697,828 people found 351 regions of DNA linked to being a morning person, yet the 5 percent carrying the most morning-leaning variants had sleep timing only about 25 minutes earlier, on average, than the 5 percent carrying the fewest, measured by activity monitors ([Jones et al., Nature Communications, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6351539/)). Hundreds of small nudges, not one switch.

Age is a second lever you don't control: lateness climbs through adolescence, peaks at about 18 for women and 19 for men, then eases through adult life (Fischer et al., above).

The third lever, light, is one you can work with. When eight people swapped ordinary life for a week of summer camping, their light exposure rose more than fourfold and their body clocks moved about two hours earlier, with the latest chronotypes moving furthest ([Wright et al., Current Biology, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020279/)). Some of what feels like your nature is your lighting.

## Are night owls less healthy?

In the UK Biobank, 433,268 adults followed for about six and a half years, definite evening types had a 10 percent higher risk of dying than definite morning types after adjustment for smoking, body weight and sleep duration, and nearly twice the odds of a psychological disorder ([Knutson and von Schantz, Chronobiology International, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29642757/)). But a 37-year Finnish study that adjusted for alcohol as well as smoking found the excess shrank to 9 percent, mainly because of those two habits, and disappeared among non-smokers who drank lightly or not at all; the authors concluded chronotype itself contributes little or nothing ([Hublin and Kaprio, Chronobiology International, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37322846/)). The genetic study found no causal effect on BMI or type 2 diabetes, though being a morning person was linked to better mental health (Jones et al., above).

The cost that shows up reliably is mismatch. In a Birmingham study that tested 56 people at 2 PM, 8 PM and 8 AM, late types were significantly worse than early types on every morning measure, from reaction time to grip strength ([Facer-Childs et al., Sports Medicine Open, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6200828/)). Many owls also pay a weekly fee, early alarms on workdays and a late clock on days off, known as [social jet lag](/blog/social-jet-lag-monday-tax). Knutson and von Schantz suggest many evening-type risk factors may trace back to exactly this: chronic misalignment between body time and the timing of work and social life.

## Can a night owl become a morning person?

Partly, and the best evidence is a small randomised trial. Facer-Childs and colleagues took 22 night owls whose habitual sleep ran from about 2:30 AM to 10:15 AM and randomly assigned them to their normal routine or a three-week intervention ([Facer-Childs et al., Sleep Medicine, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31202686/)). The intervention group was asked to get up and go to bed two to three hours earlier, maximise outdoor light in the morning and limit light in the evening, keep the same sleep times on workdays and days off, eat breakfast soon after waking and lunch at a set time, and skip dinner after 7 PM ([University of Birmingham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2019/night-owls-can-retrain-their-body-clocks-to-improve-mental-well-being-and-performance)).

Their sleep and their body clocks, measured by melatonin and morning cortisol timing, moved about two hours earlier with no loss of sleep. Self-reported depression and stress fell, and morning reaction time and grip strength improved.

**What can change:** the timing of the clock, by about an hour or two, through light and regular habits.

**What can't:** the lean underneath, meaning the genes, the long internal day and the age curve. Because light resets the clock every morning, an earlier schedule is something you keep up rather than finish, and the researchers say more work is needed on whether the change lasts. For a slower route in fifteen-minute steps, see [how to become a morning person without hating your life](/blog/become-morning-person-realistic).

## A two-week plan to wake up early as a night owl

The plan assumes your work alarm is fixed and your body has to move. Expect modest progress: the Birmingham owls needed three weeks and the full bundle to move two hours.

1. **Days 1 to 3: write it down.** Bedtime, rough time asleep, wake time and how the first hour felt, including a day off if you can. A sleep diary is also what to bring if you end up seeing a sleep doctor ([AASM Sleep Education](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/delayed-sleep-wake-phase/)).

2. **From day 1: one wake time, seven days a week.** Keep the workday alarm on weekends, or no more than an hour later; everything else hangs on it.

3. **Every morning: daylight, early.** Get outside as soon as you can after waking, for about 30 minutes, or sit by a bright window ([Nesbitt, Journal of Thoracic Disease, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803043/)). When your alarm rings before sunrise, a light box is the stand-in; [our SAD lamp guide](/blog/sad-lamp-light-therapy-guide) covers how to use one.

4. **Every evening: dim from dusk.** Overhead lights off, lamps low, screens dimmer. Cutting evening light alone can move the clock earlier (Nesbitt, above).

5. **Days 4 to 14: move bedtime 15 minutes earlier every three or four days.** [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/14295-delayed-sleep-phase-syndrome-dsps) suggests moving bedtime in small steps; morning light makes each one possible. Our [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) works backward from your wake time.

6. **Put meals, exercise and coffee on the same clock.** Breakfast soon after waking, lunch at a fixed time, dinner done by about 7 PM. In a lab study, an hour of moderate exercise at 7 AM or from 1 to 4 PM shifted the clock earlier, while the same workout from 7 to 10 PM shifted it later ([Youngstedt et al., The Journal of Physiology, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30784068/)). A double espresso's worth of caffeine three hours before bed delayed the body clock by about 40 minutes ([Burke et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26378246/)), so keep coffee to the morning.

7. **Day 14: check honestly.** Falling asleep a little earlier, first hour a little kinder? Keep going in the same steps. If two weeks changed nothing, read the next section.

## Night owl or delayed sleep phase disorder?

At the far end of the curve, late becomes a diagnosis. In delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, sleep runs two or more hours later than conventional times, falling asleep earlier and waking for work or school are both a struggle, and on your own schedule you sleep a normal amount, just late (AASM, above). The AASM estimates it may affect 7 to 16 percent of teens and young adults, and about 40 percent of people with it have a family history. The boundary with an extreme owl is hard to draw (Nesbitt, above), but Cleveland Clinic points to daytime damage: night owls don't have the severe daytime sleepiness seen with the disorder.

See a clinician if weeks of a fixed wake time and morning light haven't moved your sleep, if work, school or mood is suffering, or if you get drowsy at the wheel, and bring the diary. The AASM notes that other sleep disorders, medical and mental health conditions, medications and substance use can produce the same picture. Treatment can include timed melatonin and morning light; the AASM says melatonin dose and timing should be discussed with a sleep doctor.

## FAQ

### What time do night owls naturally wake up?

There is no official cut-off. In US diary data, the average weekend midpoint of sleep was about 3:26 AM, and researchers counted a midpoint after 5 AM as late, which with eight hours of sleep means roughly 1 AM to 9 AM or later. The night owls in the Birmingham trial averaged about 2:30 AM to 10:15 AM.

### Is being a night owl a symptom of ADHD?

Not on its own: a late body clock by itself is not a diagnostic sign. The overlap is real, though: a systematic review of 62 studies covering 4,462 people with ADHD found consistent evidence of later chronotypes and delayed body-clock markers such as melatonin onset ([Coogan and McGowan, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28064405/)). If late nights come with long-standing trouble with attention, restlessness or impulsivity, raise both with a clinician.

### Do night owls become morning people with age?

On average they drift that way, slowly. Lateness peaks at around 18 to 19 and then eases: in the US data, the average weekend midpoint for adults aged 25 to 65 sat between about 3 AM and 4 AM, moving earlier with age, with a plateau in middle age. Age softens an owl's timing, but it is not a plan.

## Back to 1:35 AM

Tonight is probably not fixable tonight. If it is already 1:35, keep the lights off and the phone where it is, and get up at 6:40 anyway: that early, slightly miserable wake time is where the shift starts. Then get outside before 8, dim the house after dinner, and do it again on Tuesday.

Your clock is not a character flaw, and it is not a life sentence. It is a day that runs a little long, corrected every morning by the light you let in, or not.
