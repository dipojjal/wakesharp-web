It is 6:15 on a Tuesday in November, still properly dark, and the alarm has just gone off. You went to bed at 11:50, put the phone down around 12:20, and the arithmetic arrives before you are upright: a little under six hours. Fine. You have been running on six since September, and if anyone asked, you would say you feel fine.

At 10:40 you read the same paragraph of an email for the third time. On the drive home the light turns green and you only notice because the driver behind you does. None of it feels like tiredness. It feels like November.

That word, fine, is the interesting part. It is just what the best-known study of chronic short sleep would lead you to expect, and it is the one reading in this story you should trust least.

**The short answer:** for most adults, no. Sleep medicine's consensus is 7 or more hours of sleep a night on a regular basis, and six falls below it. In a two-week lab study, people held to six hours in bed got steadily worse at staying attentive while their own sleepiness ratings barely moved after the first days, and six hours or less produced deficits equivalent to up to two nights without sleep. A rare few people, with specific gene variants, genuinely do well on less.

## Is 6 hours of sleep enough? What the guidelines say

In 2015 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society agreed that adults should sleep 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis to promote optimal health ([Watson et al., *Sleep*, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4434546/)). Regularly sleeping less is associated with weight gain, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and stroke, depression and a higher risk of death, and with more errors and a greater risk of accidents. The CDC's chart agrees: 7 or more hours for adults 18 to 60, 7 to 9 from 61 to 64, and 7 to 8 from 65 on ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)).

You would have company: in a CDC survey of 444,306 adults, more than a third reported less than seven hours ([Liu et al., *MMWR*, 2016](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm6506a1.htm)). Associations like the health list above come largely from population studies, which can show a link without proving a cause. For cause and effect, you need the lab.

One detail to carry through: the guidelines count sleep, while most lab studies set time in bed, and six hours between lights out and the alarm is less than six hours asleep.

## What 4, 5, 6 and 7 hours a night do to your mornings

### Four hours

Van Dongen and colleagues held healthy adults to 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed for 14 nights, compared them with a group kept awake for three nights, and tested everyone through the day ([*Sleep*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)). The deficits were dose-dependent, so of the three restricted groups, the four-hour one fared worst. In a separate study, three hours in bed sent response speed down and attention lapses up steadily for all seven days ([Belenky et al., *Journal of Sleep Research*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/)).

### Five hours

Dinges and colleagues held 16 young adults to about five hours a night for a week. Sleepiness, fatigue, confusion, tension and overall mood disturbance rose across the days, and so did lapses on the vigilance test; recovery appeared to take two full nights ([*Sleep*, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9231952/)). In Belenky's study, five hours in bed pushed response speed down to a lower plateau and raised lapses, and three recovery nights at eight hours lifted neither.

### Six hours

This is the dose Van Dongen's study is known for. Two weeks at six hours in bed produced cumulative deficits on every task, and the authors concluded that six hours or less produced deficits equivalent to up to two nights of total sleep deprivation. Their model shows why it creeps: lapses tracked the hours spent awake beyond about 15.8 a day, however those hours piled up. Sleep six and you are awake eighteen, so roughly two hours a day go on the tab.

One six-hour night is not the problem. A month of them is.

### Seven hours

Seven is where the guideline starts, and Belenky's seven-hour group is a warning against treating the floor as the target. Seven hours in bed, which is less than seven asleep, still brought response speed down to a lower plateau (lapses did not rise), and speed had not recovered after three nights at eight.

### On the road

The most practical numbers come from crashes. Compared with drivers who had slept 7 or more hours in the previous 24, an AAA Foundation analysis of police-reported crashes found 1.3 times the crash rate after 6 to 7 hours, 1.9 times after 5 to 6, 4.3 times after 4 to 5 and 11.5 times under 4 ([Tefft, AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety, 2016](https://aaafoundation.org/acute-sleep-deprivation-risk-motor-vehicle-crash-involvement/)).

## Why you stop noticing

This is the finding behind the word fine. In Van Dongen's study, sleepiness ratings jumped when the restriction began, then rose only slightly and never clearly separated the six-hour group from the four-hour group, while performance kept falling. The authors put it plainly: the ratings suggest participants were largely unaware of their growing deficits.

Belenky's team offered an explanation. Under mild to moderate restriction, the brain appears to adapt, holding performance steady at a reduced level. That reduced level feels normal because it is the only normal you remember. You judge today against yesterday, and yesterday was six hours too.

Mornings blur it further. Sleep inertia, the fog right after waking, is worse after prior sleep loss ([Hilditch and McHill, *Nature and Science of Sleep*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)), so a short night stacks a deeper fog on a smaller reserve, and [your reaction time at 7 AM](/blog/morning-reaction-time-cognition) is a worse witness than it feels.

## The rare people who really do need less

They exist. In 2009 researchers found a DEC2 gene mutation in two members of one family who habitually slept about 6.25 hours a day, against 8.06 hours for relatives without it ([He et al., *Science*, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884988/)). A 2019 study added a rare ADRB1 variant, found in a family with what researchers call natural short sleep: a lifelong tendency to sleep only 4 to 6 hours a night and to feel well rested ([Shi et al., *Neuron*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6763376/)).

These are lifelong short sleepers, found family by family, not people who trained themselves into it. If you need an alarm, two coffees and a Saturday lie-in to run on six, that pattern looks a lot more like sleep debt than a rare gene.

## How to tell whether you are getting enough sleep

- **Take a sleep vacation.** Harvard's Division of Sleep Medicine suggests about two weeks on a flexible schedule, with a consistent bedtime and no alarm ([Assess Your Sleep Needs](https://sleep.hms.harvard.edu/education-training/public-education/sleep-and-health-education-program/sleep-health-education-92)). Once the debt is paid off, you should settle into a pattern, probably between 7 and 9 hours. That is your number.
- **Check the weekend gap.** If you routinely sleep two hours longer on days off, that gap is the debt showing, and a lie-in [only repays part of it](/blog/sleep-debt-weekend-lie-ins).
- **Watch for dozing.** Nodding off in routine settings (reading, on the sofa, as a passenger) is likely a sign you are not getting enough.
- **Do the sum.** Our [sleep debt calculator](/tools/sleep-debt-calculator) compares a week of nights with your own target and shows the total shortfall.

Some patterns call for a clinician rather than an earlier bedtime. The NHS lists loud snoring, breathing that stops and starts, gasping or choking in your sleep, a headache on waking and feeling very tired during the day as symptoms of sleep apnea ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sleep-apnoea/)). The same goes for waking unrefreshed after seven to nine hours, nodding off at the wheel, or lying awake even when you give yourself the time. Six hours by choice and six hours because you cannot sleep are different problems.

## How to get from six hours to seven

1. **Keep the alarm where it is and move bedtime.** A steady wake time anchors your body clock, so the extra sleep should come from the front of the night, often out of the last hour of scrolling. Bring lights out forward by 15 minutes every few nights.
2. **Plan time in bed, not sleep.** If you take 20 minutes to drift off, seven hours asleep needs closer to seven and a half in bed. Our [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) finds the bedtime for a seven, eight or nine hour target and adds the time you take to fall asleep.
3. **Repay across the week, not on Saturday.** Thirty extra minutes a night for seven nights adds up to more than a three-hour lie-in, without pushing your body clock later.
4. **Respect the morning after a short night.** Under five hours, the crash numbers are the reason to get a ride, take transit or leave later. Save decisions you cannot undo for later in the day.
5. **Keep a two-week record.** Bedtime, wake time and one line about the morning, written before the day starts arguing with you.

## FAQ

### Is 7 hours of sleep enough?

For many adults, yes: seven hours of actual sleep meets the AASM and CDC recommendations. Treat it as the floor rather than the goal, though. Harvard's sleep division puts most adults' need at 7.5 to 8.5 hours to function optimally, and in Belenky's study even seven hours in bed slowed response speed. If you sleep seven and still need an alarm, a mid-morning coffee and a weekend lie-in, try eight for two weeks.

### Can you function on 5 hours of sleep?

You can get through a day, which is not the same as functioning well. A week near five hours raised fatigue, confusion, tension and attention lapses in Dinges's study, and drivers who had slept 4 to 5 hours in the previous 24 had 4.3 times the crash rate of those who slept 7 or more. If five is a one-off, keep the day simple and skip the drive if you can; if it is your normal, raise it with a clinician.

### Is it better to sleep 6 hours or 8?

Eight, for most adults. Six sits below the AASM and CDC recommendations; eight sits inside them. If you get to six by waking early, the hours you lose are the late ones, when you usually get more of your REM sleep ([NHLBI](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/stages-of-sleep)). Pooled cohort studies link both short and long sleep with higher mortality ([Cappuccio et al., *Sleep*, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/)), but these are questionnaire-based associations, and the consensus statement calls any risk from regularly sleeping more than nine hours uncertain for most people. If six appeals because it is four neat 90-minute cycles, [that arithmetic is weaker than it looks](/blog/sleep-cycles-90-minute-myth).

### Is 6 hours of sleep enough for a teenager?

No. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours for 13 to 18 year olds ([Paruthi et al., *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/)), and the CDC agrees for ages 13 to 17. Regularly sleeping less is associated with attention, behavior and learning problems, and with higher risks of accidents, injuries and depression. A teenager on six is not one hour short but two to four, every night.

## One more hour

The reassuring part is how unglamorous the fix is: roughly one more hour, taken from the end of the evening rather than the start of the morning, kept up long enough to learn what your mornings feel like without the debt.

Give it two weeks. If the 10:40 paragraph reads right the first time, and the green light registers before the driver behind you does, you will have your answer. It will be a better one than fine.
