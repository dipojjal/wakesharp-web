It is 2:05 on a Saturday afternoon. The week has caught up with you, the couch is right there, and you have read that 20 minutes is the correct length for a nap. You set a timer and close your eyes feeling faintly virtuous.

The timer goes off at 2:25. You silence it, grant yourself one more minute, and open your eyes at 3:40 with a cushion seam printed on your cheek. Your head is full of wet sand, you are slower than before you lay down, and you spend the next half hour in the kitchen trying to remember what you came in for. At 11:30 tonight, you will be wide awake.

That is not a discipline problem. The nap failed on two numbers: how long you slept, and which stage of sleep the alarm finally pulled you out of. Both are predictable, so both are fixable.

**The short answer:** for a quick boost in alertness, a nap should be 10 to 20 minutes of actual sleep, which usually means an alarm set 15 to 30 minutes after you lie down. Much past half an hour, you are increasingly likely to wake from deep sleep and feel worse before you feel better. Nap in the early afternoon, ideally before 3 PM, and save the 90-minute nap for days when you can afford the recovery time, such as before a night shift.

## How long should a nap be? Each length, compared

You drift through light sleep first and go deeper the longer you stay down. NIOSH, the CDC's workplace health institute, puts the deepest stage, slow-wave sleep, about an hour in, and notes that you may get there faster if you start the nap badly short on sleep ([CDC NIOSH, Nap Duration](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/05.html)). Being pulled out of that stage is what makes the fog worst.

The cleanest test of nap length is a lab study by Brooks and Lack. They cut 24 good sleepers who did not usually nap to about five hours of sleep, then gave them a 3 PM nap of exactly 5, 10, 20 or 30 minutes of sleep, or none, and measured them over the following hours ([Brooks & Lack, *Sleep*, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/)).

| Minutes asleep | What it does | Grogginess after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Few benefits over no nap | Little |
| 10 | Immediate improvements in sleepiness, fatigue, vigor and performance, some lasting up to about 155 minutes | Little |
| 20 | Improvements that appeared about 35 minutes after waking and lasted until about 125 minutes after | Brief |
| 30 | Impaired alertness first, then improvements lasting up to about 155 minutes | Noticeable |
| 60 | Likely to end in deep sleep, where NIOSH says functioning might deteriorate considerably | The worst exit |
| 90 | Roughly one sleep cycle, so a better chance of surfacing from light sleep | Less, if the cycle lines up |

The first four rows are measurements from one study of young, good sleepers; the last two are what sleep architecture predicts. Treat the minutes as a guide, not a law. Mainstream advice lands nearby: Cleveland Clinic calls 15 to 20 minutes best for alertness ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/are-long-naps-bad-for-you-and-if-so-why)), and Mayo Clinic calls 20 to 30 ideal and suggests not regularly napping longer than 30 ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/napping/art-20048319)).

Much of that spread is probably minutes asleep versus minutes on the couch. Research counts the first; your alarm counts the second. That is why NIOSH recommends a nap of under 20 minutes for daytime workers, with an alarm set for 15 to 30.

## Why do I feel worse after a nap?

Because you woke up in the wrong place. The heavy, slow feeling after a long nap is sleep inertia, the same fog that follows a brutal morning alarm, and it is worst when you surface from slow-wave sleep. Two things push a nap there: it ran long (the silenced alarm is the usual suspect), or you started it short on sleep, so deep sleep came early. Mayo Clinic says the grogginess often lifts within about 35 minutes, which is a long time to be bad at things. The mechanism, and what shortens it, is in [our guide to sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy).

## When to break the 20-minute rule

Short naps are the default because they are cheap. Long naps cost more, and sometimes they are worth it.

- **Before a night shift.** NIOSH calls this a prophylactic nap, usually a few hours long, whose deep sleep reduces the buildup of sleep pressure before you stay up all night. Nurses who napped from 3:30 to 5 PM felt significantly more alert in the second half of their first night shift ([CDC NIOSH, Before Nights](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/06.html)).
- **After a very short night.** Cleveland Clinic describes a recovery nap of 20 to 60 minutes, with two warnings: it should not become routine, and no nap should stretch past an hour. Leave a buffer for the fog.
- **For learning.** Naps of 60 to 90 minutes containing both slow-wave and REM sleep improved a visual learning task about as much as a full night did ([Mednick et al., *Nature Neuroscience*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12819785/)). That is one task, not a general memory upgrade.

Ninety minutes approximates one sleep cycle, so you are more likely to wake from light sleep, but cycles vary by person and through the night ([the 90-minute myth](/blog/sleep-cycles-90-minute-myth) has the numbers). Treat it as a bet, placed where 20 minutes of fog would not ruin anything.

## Does a coffee nap work?

It sounds invented for a productivity newsletter, but the coffee nap has a small, decent evidence base. Drink a coffee, then nap for 15 to 20 minutes straight away. Caffeine takes a while to absorb: in one controlled study, caffeine from coffee peaked in saliva about 40 minutes after drinking ([Liguori et al., *Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior*, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9329065/)), so it is still climbing when the alarm goes off.

Reyner and Horne gave 12 sleepy volunteers a 30-minute break before a two-hour afternoon simulator drive. With 200 mg of caffeine and a short nap in the break, driving incidents fell to 9 percent of the placebo level, against 34 percent for caffeine alone ([Reyner & Horne, *Psychophysiology*, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9401427/)). In 10 young adults, Hayashi and colleagues found 200 mg of caffeine followed by a 20-minute nap the most effective condition for sleepiness and performance over the next hour; a minute of bright light after the nap was comparable, except on performance ([Hayashi et al., *Clinical Neurophysiology*, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14652086/)).

So yes, with caveats: the studies are small, and caffeine has a long tail, so a 4 PM coffee nap can cost you tonight ([the caffeine cutoff arithmetic](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-time-sleep) applies in full). If you are pregnant, take regular medication, or have a heart rhythm or anxiety condition, ask your clinician before using caffeine as a tool. And if you are drowsy at the wheel, pull over somewhere safe: the simulator study tested a break, not a way to keep driving.

## When to nap, and when to skip it

NIOSH describes a secondary window of sleep pressure around 2 to 4 PM, and says it is harder to fall asleep in late morning and in the 1 to 3 hours before your usual bedtime ([CDC NIOSH, Nap Times](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/04.html)). Mayo Clinic warns that napping after 3 PM can make it harder to sleep soundly at night. A brief nap leaves tonight alone, NIOSH says, because it does not reduce the buildup of sleep pressure. A long one does, which is why it helps before a night shift and hurts before a normal bedtime.

If you have insomnia, the usual advice is to skip naps altogether. The NHS puts it bluntly: do not nap during the day ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/)).

And notice when the need changes. Mayo advises talking to a clinician if you need to nap more than usual, especially if you also wake up tired in the morning, since a medicine, a sleep disorder or another condition can be behind it. Cleveland Clinic adds that a real struggle to stay awake can point to narcolepsy. If you doze off without meaning to, at the wheel above all, book that appointment now.

## How to take a nap that actually ends

1. **Choose the length before you lie down.** Ten to 20 minutes for alertness; 90 only when you can absorb some fog; nothing in between without budgeted recovery time.
2. **Set the alarm for the nap and the time it takes you to drift off.** For a short nap, 15 to 30 minutes. Our [nap timer](/tools/nap-timer) runs in a browser tab with 10, 20 and 30 minute presets; like any timer, it measures elapsed time, not sleep.
3. **Make the room easy.** Mayo's list: quiet, dark, a comfortable temperature, and fewer distractions such as screens.
4. **Have the coffee first, if it is early enough.**
5. **Do not assume you never slept.** NIOSH lists fragmented images, a sudden twitch or a falling sensation as signs of light sleep.
6. **Plan the first 15 minutes after.** Daylight, a short walk, water, and, as Mayo advises, time to wake up before anything that needs a quick or sharp response: no driving, no decisions you cannot take back.

## A nap alarm you will actually get up for

The 20-minute rule has one weak point, and it is not the science. It is the moment the alarm rings and you, warm and groggy, negotiate. One tap silences almost any timer, and "one more minute" is how the 20-minute nap in the opening became 95.

## FAQ

### Is a 20 minute nap too long?

No, for most adults it sits at the top of the short range. In Brooks and Lack's study, 20 minutes of sleep brought benefits that appeared about 35 minutes after waking and lasted until about two hours after the nap, while 10 minutes paid off at once. If 20 leaves you groggy, try 10 to 15. The figure means minutes asleep, so the alarm can sit closer to 30.

### Is a 60 minute nap okay?

Occasionally, if you can live with the exit. An hour is roughly when NIOSH expects slow-wave sleep, so waking then tends to bring the roughest grogginess, and Cleveland Clinic advises keeping naps under an hour. It can make sense after a very short night or before a night shift, with half an hour of buffer afterwards. A two-hour nap is still a nap, but Cleveland Clinic says naps past an hour disrupt most people's night sleep.

### What is the 30-60-90 nap rule?

It is an internet rule of thumb rather than a clinical guideline: nap under 30 minutes or for about 90, and avoid the middle, where you are most likely to wake from deep sleep. The logic matches how NIOSH describes sleep deepening over the first hour. Its weak point is the 90, because cycle length varies, so treat it as a bet you can afford to lose.

### Is it bad to nap every day?

A short daily nap looks fine for most people; a long one deserves a closer look. Pooling 11 prospective studies of more than 150,000 people, Yamada and colleagues linked naps of an hour or more a day to higher rates of cardiovascular disease and death from any cause, while shorter naps showed no significant link ([Yamada et al., *Sleep*, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26158892/)). That is observational data, and a long daily nap may signal poor night sleep or illness rather than cause anything. A 2023 genetic analysis of UK Biobank data even tied habitual napping to a modestly larger total brain volume, though not to better reaction time or visual memory ([Paz et al., *Sleep Health*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37344293/)).

## Decide the nap before you take it

A good nap is small and deliberate: 10 to 20 minutes of sleep, early in the afternoon, with an alarm that actually ends it. The nap in the opening was not too much rest. It was the wrong amount, at the wrong depth, at the wrong time, and all three are settled before you lie down, not after the alarm rings. Pick the length, add your falling-asleep minutes, and put the alarm where you have to get up to deal with it.
