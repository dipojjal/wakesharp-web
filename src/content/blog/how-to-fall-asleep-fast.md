It is 11:52 on a Sunday night. You have been in bed since 11:15, the alarm is set for 6:20, and you are lying very still doing arithmetic: asleep by midnight is six hours twenty, asleep by half past is five fifty. You try to empty your head, and it replays something you said in Thursday's meeting.

At 12:09 you check the phone. It costs you a bright screen, a shipping notification and the news that it is 12:09.

None of this is a character flaw. Sleep is not something you do on purpose, like opening a door; it happens when the right conditions line up, and nearly everything in that scene (the effort, the clock, the screen) pushes them apart.

**The short answer:** a healthy time to fall asleep is typically 15 to 20 minutes, so falling asleep fast is mostly about removing what slows you down. Go to bed only when you are genuinely sleepy, and if you are still awake after about 20 minutes, get up and do something dull in dim light until you are. In bed, slow your breathing to around six breaths a minute, relax your muscles or shuffle your thoughts. The military method and 4-7-8 breathing repackage those ingredients, with little direct evidence of their own.

## Why you can't fall asleep when you're exhausted

Sleep comes when two things agree: sleep drive, the need for sleep that builds while you are awake, and a body clock that says it is night. Relaxation techniques can make sleep more likely, a Cleveland Clinic sleep psychologist notes, but they are probably not what puts you to sleep ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/military-sleep-method)).

Against both stands arousal: in the body, tense muscles, a higher temperature and a quicker heartbeat ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/how-to-fall-asleep-fast)); in the mind, replaying and planning. Three habits feed it.

**Trying harder.** With calm music playing, people told to fall asleep as fast as possible did so sooner than people told to sleep whenever they liked. With John Philip Sousa marches playing, the ones hurrying took longer ([Behaviour Research and Therapy, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8826759/)). Effort helps a quiet mind and backfires on a busy one.

**Watching the clock.** People told to watch a clock while trying to sleep worried more and took longer to drop off, and those with insomnia overestimated how long they took to fall asleep by more than people watching a neutral display ([Tang and colleagues, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16793001/)).

**Scrolling.** In an inpatient study, about four hours of reading on a light-emitting e-reader before bed added nearly ten minutes to falling asleep, compared with a printed book, and suppressed evening melatonin by more than half ([PNAS, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4313820/)). A phone is also endlessly interesting.

## How to fall asleep fast: 9 methods, best evidence first

The first seven have direct research behind them; the last two are the internet's favorites.

### 1. Go to bed sleepy, not on schedule

Sleepy means heavy-lidded, not just tired. Stimulus control, a core insomnia technique, means going to bed only when sleepy and getting up at the same time every morning, however the night went ([AASM guideline, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)). If you often lie awake early in the night, Cleveland Clinic's sleep psychologist suggests moving bedtime later in steps of 30 to 60 minutes. The [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) works backward from your fixed wake time.

### 2. Can't fall asleep? Get up after about 20 minutes

Go to another room, keep the light low and do something calm, such as reading a paper book, until you feel drowsy, then go back to bed ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/how-to-fall-asleep-fast)). Lying there struggling teaches your brain that bed is for lying awake frustrated; leaving breaks the link. Estimate the 20 minutes instead of timing them. This get-up rule feels backward, and it is part of the treatment insomnia guidelines put first.

### 3. Take a warm shower or bath one to two hours before bed

A meta-analysis linked water at 40 to 42.5°C (104 to 108.5°F) with better self-rated sleep, and a shower or bath one to two hours before bed, for as little as 10 minutes, with falling asleep significantly faster ([Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31102877/)). The likely mechanism: warm water sends blood to the palms and soles, they shed heat, and core temperature drops further. The research is thin, and a hot shower at 11:50 is a different experiment.

### 4. Write tomorrow's to-do list

In a sleep-lab study, 57 young adults spent five minutes before bed listing either tasks still to do or tasks already done. The to-do group fell asleep in about 16 minutes on average, the done-list group in about 25, and more specific lists meant faster sleep ([Scullin and colleagues, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758411/)). Healthy volunteers, so call it promising; it costs five minutes and a pen.

### 5. Slow your breathing to six breaths a minute

In a study of 14 people with insomnia, 20 minutes of paced breathing at six breaths a minute before bed shortened the time to fall asleep, cut awakenings and improved sleep efficiency, measured by polysomnography ([Psychophysiology, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25234581/)). Try breathing in for about four seconds and out for about six.

### 6. Tense, then release (progressive muscle relaxation)

Work from face to feet: tense a muscle group for about ten seconds, let go, breathe and move on, skipping anywhere that hurts. The AASM gives relaxation therapy, which includes this, a conditional recommendation for chronic insomnia. A meta-analysis of 31 randomized trials found it improved sleep quality, though its authors caution that effects that large are unusual for a technique measured mostly by questionnaire ([Journal of Psychosomatic Research, 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022399926000474)).

### 7. Shuffle your thoughts (cognitive shuffling)

Pick a neutral word, such as *blanket*. Then, letter by letter, think of things that start with that letter and picture each for a few seconds: bicycle, bucket, banana. Luc Beaudoin of Simon Fraser University designed this cognitive shuffle to mimic the scattered imagery of a brain drifting off, leaving no room for worry.

An absorbing imagery task helped 41 people with insomnia fall asleep faster, with fewer and less distressing unwanted thoughts, than no instructions ([Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11863237/)). The shuffle itself rests mainly on a 2016 poster from Beaudoin's group, in which 154 students with racing minds did about as well with a shuffling app as with structured problem-solving earlier in the evening ([SLEEP 2016](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/16196/Abstract%20Beaudoin%20Digdon%20ONeill%20Rachor%202016%20Serial%20Diverse%20Imagining%20Task-A%20New%20Remedy%20for%20Bedtime%20Complaints%20of%20Worrying%20and%20Other%20Sleep-Disruptive%20Mental%20Activity.pdf)). There was no do-nothing group, so it is plausible rather than proven.

## The military method and 4-7-8: where they came from

### 8. Does the military sleep method work?

Relax your face, drop your shoulders and arms, breathe deeply to loosen your chest, relax your legs from the hips down, then picture something calm, such as a canoe on a still river. It is usually traced to the track coach Lloyd "Bud" Winter's 1981 book *Relax and Win: Championship Performance*, which describes teaching it to Navy pilots in training during the Second World War. The famous figure, 96 percent asleep within two minutes after six weeks of practice, is Winter's own account, not a study. Cleveland Clinic notes that reports conflict on where it started and whether the military teaches it, and that no studies have tested the method itself ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/military-sleep-method)).

Its ingredients (breathing, muscle relaxation, imagery) are close cousins of methods 5 to 7, which have evidence. The two-minute promise has none, and checking whether it has worked yet is clock-watching with extra steps.

### 9. What is the 4-7-8 breathing method?

With the tip of your tongue behind your top front teeth, breathe out through your mouth with a whoosh, in through your nose for a count of four, hold for seven, then out through your mouth for eight. Do four cycles. The integrative medicine physician Andrew Weil popularized it, drawing on yogic pranayama, and you may feel lightheaded at first ([Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/4-7-8-breathing)).

In the study Cleveland Clinic cites, heart rate and systolic blood pressure fell after 43 healthy young adults tried it; sleep was not measured ([Physiological Reports, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35822447/)). In a small trial of 48 people with tinnitus, six weeks of practice lowered self-rated insomnia severity compared with an information session alone ([Brain and Behavior, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41676854/)). Neither shows it gets you to sleep faster tonight. If the hold makes you anxious, plain slow breathing is the version measured against sleep.

## When it's insomnia, CBT-I is the answer

In 2020, 14.5 percent of US adults had trouble falling asleep most days or every day in the past month ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db436.htm)). At least three nights a week for at least three months meets the usual bar for chronic insomnia ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/12119-insomnia)), and the American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment for every adult with it ([Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136449/)). CBT-I pairs the get-up rule with sleep restriction (a temporary, structured limit on time in bed) and work on the thoughts that keep you awake, typically over four to eight sessions ([AASM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)), in person or through an online program ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/)).

See a doctor if poor sleep makes your days hard to cope with or changing your habits has not helped. Mention loud snoring, gasping for air or breathing that stops and starts in your sleep, possible signs of sleep apnea ([NHLBI](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea/symptoms)), or an irresistible urge to move your legs when you lie still, the hallmark of restless legs syndrome ([NINDS](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/restless-legs-syndrome)). And do not drive when you feel sleepy.

## FAQ

### What helps you sleep quicker?

Going to bed only when sleepy, keeping a fixed wake time and getting up after about 20 minutes awake have the best support. Add a warm shower one to two hours before bed and a quick to-do list, then slow breathing, muscle relaxation or cognitive shuffling with the light off. If outside noise keeps you up, earplugs or a steady background sound can mask it; see our guide to [white, pink and brown noise](/blog/white-pink-brown-noise-sleep).

### How can I fall asleep in 10 minutes?

You may not need to. A healthy time to fall asleep is typically 15 to 20 minutes, and regularly dropping off in under 10 can be a sign of sleep deprivation ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/how-to-fall-asleep-fast)). Cleveland Clinic's sleep psychologist says taking 20 or 30 minutes is not something most people need to fix. Instead, stop adding time: go to bed sleepy, hide the clock and use whichever technique calms you.

### What will knock me out to sleep?

Nothing safely works like an off switch. Alcohol speeds up falling asleep but disrupts the second half of the night ([Ebrahim and colleagues, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). For chronic insomnia, the AASM suggests clinicians not use diphenhydramine (an antihistamine found in several sleep aids), melatonin, valerian or tryptophan ([Sateia and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27998379/)), and the NHS says sleeping pills are prescribed for a few days or weeks at most because of side effects and the risk of dependence. If you are reaching for something most nights, talk to a clinician.
