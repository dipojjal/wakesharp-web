It is 3:15 on a Wednesday afternoon, and you have just read the same row of a spreadsheet for the fourth time. You were in bed by 10:45 and, as far as you know, slept straight through. The alarm went at 6:50, you were up on the second try, and there have been two coffees since. You still feel as if someone let the air out of you.

So you type "why am i always tired" into a search bar, and the internet offers everything from a vitamin to a rare disease. It has been like this since September.

**The short answer:** most often, you are always tired because you are not sleeping enough, or not at regular times. Adults need 7 or more hours of sleep a night, and more than a third of US adults report usually getting less. If you sleep seven hours or more on a steady schedule and are still tired after a few weeks, the usual medical causes are sleep apnea, low iron or anemia, thyroid problems, depression or anxiety, and medications, and a doctor can check for them with questions, a blood test or a sleep study.

## What does fatigue mean, and is it the same as sleepy?

Not quite. Sleep researchers treat sleepiness and fatigue as related but distinct, though everyday speech merges both into one word, "tired" ([Shen et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16376590/)). Sleepiness is a pull toward sleep: heavy eyelids at 3 PM, nodding off in a warm room. Fatigue is a lack of energy that rest does not seem to fix.

The line is blurry, but useful: sleepiness points first at your nights, while fatigue that a good night does not touch points more toward the body and mood.

## Why do I wake up tired every morning? Four everyday reasons

### 1. You are not getting enough sleep

The likeliest answer, and the least interesting. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html) recommends 7 or more hours for adults up to 60, and in its survey data more than a third of US adults said they usually sleep less ([MMWR, 2016](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm6506a1.htm)). Count sleep, not time in bed. And do not trust how you feel: people kept to six hours in bed for two weeks got steadily worse on every test while their sleepiness ratings barely rose after the first days ([Van Dongen et al., 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/); more in [Is 6 Hours of Sleep Enough?](/blog/is-6-hours-of-sleep-enough)).

**What helps:** count back from your wake time, add the time it takes you to fall asleep, and keep that bedtime for two weeks before judging.

### 2. Your sleep times wander

Getting up at 6:45 on weekdays and 10 on Saturdays moves your body clock into a different time zone every weekend. A National Sleep Foundation panel that reviewed 63 publications concluded that regular sleep timing matters for health, safety and performance in its own right ([Sletten et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37684151/)).

**What helps:** one wake time, seven days a week, with bedtime set to fit. The panel found weekend catch-up sleep may help after a short week, so a lie-in is a patch, not a crime.

### 3. You are judging the day by its first half hour

The fog right after waking is sleep inertia, and it is normal. It can lift in as little as 15 minutes, but full recovery seems to take at least an hour, and it hits harder after short sleep or a wake-up in your biological night ([Hilditch and McHill, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)). Plenty of people who call themselves always tired are really describing 6:50 AM.

**What helps:** judge your energy at 10 AM, not at the alarm. Daylight, movement and a task that makes you think help the fog lift sooner, as our [guide to sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy) explains.

### 4. Caffeine and alcohol are editing your night

Both cost sleep in ways that are easy to miss. In a controlled study, 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bed cut more than an hour of measured sleep, yet participants' sleep diaries did not reliably register the loss ([Drake et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)). Alcohol, at every dose, helps you fall asleep faster and then disrupts the second half of the night, a review found ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). Either way, the night looks fine on the clock.

**What helps:** move your last coffee to early afternoon ([the half-life arithmetic is here](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-time-sleep)), and check whether your worst mornings follow the evenings you drank.

## Why am I always tired, even after enough sleep? Six more reasons

### 5. Sleep apnea

In sleep apnea, breathing stops and restarts many times during sleep, which can keep your body from getting enough oxygen ([NHLBI](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea)). Snoring and gasping are the famous signs, but the [symptom list](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea/symptoms) also includes daytime sleepiness, fatigue, headaches, a dry mouth and waking at night to urinate, with fatigue, headaches and insomnia more common in women. Its bleakest line: you may not know until someone tells you.

**What helps:** ask whoever shares your room what your nights sound like. A doctor can arrange a sleep study, and breathing devices such as CPAP are a common treatment.

### 6. Low iron or anemia

Your body needs iron to make healthy red blood cells, and fatigue tops the [NHLBI's symptom list](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/anemia/iron-deficiency-anemia) for iron-deficiency anemia, though mild cases may cause none. Heavy periods, bleeding in the digestive tract and problems absorbing iron raise the risk. Low iron may matter even before anemia: in a trial of 198 menstruating women with unexplained fatigue who were low in iron but not anemic, fatigue scores fell by almost half over 12 weeks on iron, against 29 percent on placebo ([Vaucher et al., CMAJ, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/)).

**What helps:** a blood test before any supplement. If your iron is low, why it is low matters too.

### 7. Thyroid problems

Thyroid hormones control how your body uses energy. When the gland makes too little, the [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/hypothyroidism) lists fatigue, weight gain, trouble tolerating cold, dry skin or hair, aches and depression among the symptoms. An underactive thyroid is much more common in women and after 60, and the NIDDK is refreshingly blunt that fatigue and weight gain are common and do not necessarily mean a thyroid problem. An overactive thyroid can cause tiredness too ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tiredness-and-fatigue/)).

**What helps:** a blood test, since the diagnosis cannot rest on symptoms alone. An underactive thyroid can usually be fully controlled with medicine.

### 8. Depression or anxiety

Depression is not only a mood. [NIMH's symptom list](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression) includes fatigue, lack of energy or feeling slowed down, and trouble sleeping, early waking or oversleeping; for a diagnosis, symptoms last most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks. [Generalized anxiety](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/generalized-anxiety-disorder-gad) can bring poor sleep and fatigue on top of the worry. NIMH adds that thyroid disorders and some medications can mimic depression.

**What helps:** tell your doctor about your mood as well as your energy; most people with depression benefit from treatment. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US.

### 9. Medications

Plenty of medicines can cause fatigue. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/21206-fatigue) names benzodiazepines, sedative sleeping pills, anti-anxiety drugs, antipsychotics, opioids, anticonvulsants, beta-blockers and over-the-counter antihistamines. The clue is timing: tiredness that began with a new prescription or dose.

**What helps:** ask your pharmacist or prescriber, and do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

### 10. Too little movement

Cleveland Clinic counts an inactive lifestyle among the causes of fatigue. In a trial, 36 sedentary young adults with persistent fatigue and no diagnosed condition exercised three times a week for six weeks: low and moderate intensity raised energy about equally, low intensity eased fatigue more, and neither change depended on getting fitter ([Puetz et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18277063/)). The wider evidence is thinner: a meta-analysis found a modest average benefit, and none in placebo-controlled studies of exercise alone ([Puetz et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073524/)).

**What helps:** start smaller than feels worthwhile, such as an easy daily walk. But if effort reliably leaves you worse for a day or more, see a doctor before exercising through it.

## Keep a one-week tiredness log

For seven days, write down:

- bedtime, rough time asleep, wake time and any night waking
- your last caffeine and any alcohol, with times
- naps and exercise
- energy at 10 AM and 3 PM, from 1 to 10, and whether it felt like sleepiness or fatigue
- mood, in a word
- anything a bed partner noticed: snoring, gasping, pauses
- medicines, supplements and, if you menstruate, whether your period is heavy

Our [sleep debt calculator](/tools/sleep-debt-calculator) totals seven nights against your target and shows how much they vary. Short or wandering nights point to reasons 1 to 4. Seven or more steady hours and still flat points to the rest.

## When should I see a doctor about being tired?

Book an appointment if you have been tired for a few weeks without knowing why, if it is affecting your daily life, if it comes with symptoms such as weight loss or mood changes, or if someone has told you that you gasp, snort or choke in your sleep. Those are the [NHS criteria](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tiredness-and-fatigue/).

Seek care right away if tiredness comes with chest pain or shortness of breath, an unusually fast, slow or irregular heartbeat, a headache or vision problems, vomiting, muscle weakness, or thoughts of harming yourself or others ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/21206-fatigue)).

And if you have nodded off at the wheel, or keep drifting from your lane or missing exits, stop driving drowsy now, not after the appointment. In a CDC survey of more than 147,000 adults, 4.2 percent said they had fallen asleep while driving in the previous 30 days ([MMWR, 2013](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6151a1.htm)).

## FAQ

### Why am I tired after 8 hours of sleep?

Usually because eight hours in bed is not eight hours of good sleep. Late caffeine, alcohol, a wandering schedule and sleep apnea can all break up a night that looks long enough, and the CDC counts feeling tired even after enough sleep as a sign of poor sleep quality. If the tiredness lasts all day for weeks, see a doctor.

### What deficiency makes you always tired?

Iron is the one most worth asking about: low iron may cause fatigue even before it becomes anemia, and heavy periods raise the risk. Cleveland Clinic notes that vitamin D and B12 deficiencies are often responsible for fatigue too. A blood test shows which, if any, applies, which beats guessing with supplements.

### What are signs of chronic fatigue?

Fatigue is usually called chronic once it has lasted six months. The illness often meant by the phrase, ME/CFS, has a pattern, according to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/signs-symptoms/index.html): a much lower ability to do things than before, with fatigue that rest does not relieve, lasting six months or more; symptoms that worsen after physical or mental effort, sometimes for days; unrefreshing sleep; and memory and thinking problems or trouble being upright. If that sounds familiar, see a doctor and mention the crashes after effort.

### Can dehydration cause tiredness?

A little. In a lab study, 25 young women who lost about 1.4 percent of their body weight in fluid reported more fatigue, less vigor, poorer concentration and more headaches, though most thinking tests were unaffected ([Armstrong et al., Journal of Nutrition, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22190027/)). Drink enough, but water is unlikely to explain weeks of tiredness.

## Start with the boring answer

Most of the time, the answer to "why am I always tired" is not exotic. It is six and a half hours a night, a Saturday that starts at 10, a 4 PM coffee and a glass of wine to wind down. All four are yours to change, starting tonight.

Give it a couple of weeks of steady, sufficient sleep and an honest log. If you are still dragging, the log has done its other job: it has turned "I'm tired all the time" into something a doctor can work with.
