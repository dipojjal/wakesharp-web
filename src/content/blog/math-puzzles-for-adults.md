It is 6:48 on a Tuesday, and you are doing the easiest sum of your day. The train leaves at 7:26, the walk to the station takes 14 minutes, and you want 2 more for a coffee on the way. You get 7:16. Then 7:08. On the third pass, holding the numbers like a stack of plates, you land on 7:10, which is right, and which you would have produced in two seconds at ten in the morning.

You did not get less intelligent overnight. The part of your brain that juggles numbers was not fully online yet, and arithmetic is unusually good at showing that, as [our piece on math alarms and morning grogginess](/blog/math-alarm-science-solving-beats-snoozing) explains. That is the honest case for a quick math puzzle: it gives you a fair read on where your head is. This week a few will also get tried at a long table, between the turkey and the pie.

**The short answer:** good math puzzles for adults are short, have one answer you can check, and make you hold a few numbers in your head at once: arithmetic chains, number patterns, quick estimates and logic questions. The twelve below take under two minutes each, with answers after them. Practice makes you better at puzzles like these, but there is little evidence the benefit spreads to thinking in general, or that a puzzle clears morning grogginess faster, so treat them as practice and as a read on how awake you are.

## What makes a math puzzle hard

Mostly three things, and none of them is intelligence in the abstract.

**Carrying.** Mental arithmetic runs on working memory, the small scratchpad that holds numbers while you work on them. In experiments at Ghent University, each extra borrow or carry, and a larger carried value, made problems harder and leaned more heavily on working memory ([Imbo, Vandierendonck and Vergauwe, Psychological Research, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16622702/)). That is why 52 minus 27 feels harder than 58 minus 23, though both are "two-digit subtraction."

**Nerves.** Math anxiety competes for the same space. People high in it had smaller working memory spans, especially on an arithmetic-based span task, and made more errors and slowed down when adding while holding something else in mind, which the authors read as a transitory disruption of working memory ([Ashcraft and Kirk, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11409101/)). Worth remembering before you put your brother-in-law on the spot.

**The first answer.** Some puzzles are built so that a wrong answer arrives instantly and feels finished. The best known is the bat-and-ball question from Shane Frederick's three-item Cognitive Reflection Test. Even at MIT, only 48 percent of the people he tested got all three items right ([Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2005](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/089533005775196732)). Puzzle 10 is a holiday version. What it trains is not arithmetic. It is checking.

## 12 math puzzles for adults (answers below)

Try each one in your head before you look. Most take under a minute, and none should need paper.

### Arithmetic chains

1. Start with 7. Double it, add 6, divide by 4, then multiply by 9.
2. Multiply 25 by 16, divide by 8, add 50, then take away 37.
3. A gravy recipe for 8 people uses 3 cups of stock. How much do you need for 20?

### Number patterns

4. What comes next: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?
5. What comes next: 3, 4, 7, 11, 18, 29, ?
6. What comes next: 5, 10, 8, 16, 14, 28, ?

### Estimation

7. Roughly how many minutes old is someone on their 30th birthday?
8. What is an 18 percent tip on a $64 bill?
9. The drive to your in-laws' is 287 miles and you average 62 miles an hour. Can you make it in four and a half hours?

### Logic

10. A slice of pie and a scoop of ice cream cost $5.50 together. The pie costs $5.00 more than the ice cream. What does the ice cream cost?
11. Twelve people at dinner each clink glasses once with every other person. How many clinks is that?
12. You have a 5-cup measure, a 3-cup measure and a big pot of stock. How do you measure exactly 4 cups?

## Answers

1. **45.** 14, then 20, then 5, then 45.
2. **63.** 25 x 16 is 400 (halve and double: 50 x 8, then 100 x 4). Then 50, 100 and 63.
3. **7.5 cups.** Twenty people is two and a half batches, and 2.5 x 3 is 7.5.
4. **42.** The gaps grow by 2 (4, 6, 8, 10), so the next one is 12. Or: 1 x 2, 2 x 3, on up to 6 x 7.
5. **47.** Each number is the sum of the two before it, and 18 and 29 make 47.
6. **26.** Double, take away 2, repeat: 28 minus 2 is 26.
7. **About 16 million.** Thirty years is close to 11,000 days of 1,440 minutes each, about 15.8 million minutes. Anything from 15 to 16 million is a good estimate.
8. **$11.52.** 10 percent is $6.40, so 20 percent is $12.80. Take away 2 percent, $1.28.
9. **Not quite.** 62 x 4.5 is 279 miles, leaving 8 miles, about 8 more minutes: roughly 4 hours 38 minutes before any stops. Take the stops.
10. **25 cents.** Then the pie is $5.25 and the total $5.50. The answer that jumps out, 50 cents, makes the pie $5.50 and the total $6.00. (Yes, it is very cheap ice cream.)
11. **66.** Each of the 12 clinks with 11 others, which counts every clink twice: 12 x 11 is 132, and half of that is 66.
12. **Fill, pour, empty, pour, fill, top up.** Fill the 5-cup and pour it into the 3-cup, leaving 2 cups. Empty the 3-cup and pour in the 2 cups. Refill the 5-cup and top up the 3-cup, which takes exactly 1 cup and leaves 4 in the 5-cup.

## How to get better at mental math: four tricks

Practice changes how you solve, not only how fast. In an imaging study at Innsbruck, after five training sessions the practiced problems drew less on frontal areas linked to working memory and executive control, and practiced multiplication shifted toward plain retrieval from memory ([Ischebeck and colleagues, NeuroImage, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16413795/)). The tricks below help with the problems you cannot remember: they take the carrying out.

### 1. Multiply by 11: put the sum in the middle

For a two-digit number, add the digits and write the sum between them: 36 x 11 is 3, then 9, then 6, so 396. If the sum reaches 10, carry the 1 to the left: 78 x 11 is 7, 15, 8, which becomes 858.

### 2. Halve one number, double the other

The product stays the same and one side turns round: 16 x 25 is 8 x 50, or 4 x 100, so 400. 35 x 14 is 70 x 7, so 490. It works best when one number is even and the other ends in 5.

### 3. Round, then pay it back

Round to a friendly number and correct afterward. 49 x 6 is 50 x 6, minus 6: 294. To add 398 and 257, add 400 and take 2 back off: 655. Most of the carrying disappears, and carrying is where the Ghent experiments found the cost.

### 4. Cast out nines to check an answer

This one checks rather than calculates. Add a number's digits until one digit is left, counting 9 as 0. Reduce both numbers you multiplied, multiply the results, reduce again, and compare with your answer reduced the same way. Is 47 x 23 = 1,081? 47 reduces to 2 (4 and 7 make 11, then 2), 23 to 5, and 2 x 5 is 10, which reduces to 1. So does 1,081. Had you written 1,071, its digits make 9, which counts as 0: look again. The catch is that swapped digits slip through, so 1,018 would pass too.

## Do math puzzles help your brain?

They help your brain do math puzzles. Beyond that, the evidence thins quickly.

A comprehensive review of brain training examined the studies that leading brain-training companies cite in their own support. It found extensive evidence that training improves the trained tasks, less for closely related tasks, and little for distantly related tasks or everyday thinking ([Simons and colleagues, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697851/)).

The encouraging studies are mostly snapshots. In PROTECT, an online study of 19,078 healthy adults aged 50 to 93, how often people did number puzzles was significantly linked to their scores on all 14 cognitive measures ([Brooker and colleagues, International Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30746778/)). A snapshot cannot say whether puzzles sharpen people or sharp people choose puzzles. A Scottish study that followed 498 people born in 1936 for 15 years gets closer: intellectual engagement, problem solving most of all, went with a higher level of ability in later life but not with a slower rate of decline ([Staff and colleagues, BMJ, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30530522/)).

As for the morning: a 2019 review of sleep inertia reports no clear evidence fully supporting any remedy used after waking for an immediate, objective effect ([Hilditch and McHill, Nature and Science of Sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)). A puzzle at 6:48 does not shorten the fog; it shows it to you. Do a chain like puzzle 1, with a new starting number, when you get up and again half an hour later. If the second try is clearly easier, the difference was grogginess, and the first important thing of your day belongs after it. How long the fog lasts is covered in [what happens in the first 30 minutes](/blog/first-30-minutes-brain-after-waking).

## FAQ

### What are good math puzzles for adults?

Short ones with a single answer you can check and a step that makes you hold numbers in your head: arithmetic chains, number sequences, quick estimates and logic questions such as the bat-and-ball problem. Mix the types: chains load working memory, estimates reward rounding, and logic questions punish the first answer that comes to mind. If a puzzle needs paper, it is a different hobby.

### How can I get better at mental math?

Practice the kinds of problems you want to be quick at until answers come from memory rather than calculation; in the Innsbruck study, brain scans showed that shift for multiplication after five training sessions. Learn one or two tricks that take the carrying out, such as rounding and paying back, and check answers that matter by casting out nines. A few puzzles a day is an easy way to get the repetitions in.

### Can math puzzles prevent dementia?

Nobody has shown that they can. A 2017 National Academies report called the evidence for cognitive training "encouraging but inconclusive" for delaying or slowing age-related decline, and found no evidence at the time that it prevents or delays mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's-type dementia ([Preventing Cognitive Decline and Dementia](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24782/chapter/2)). Do puzzles because you enjoy them. If someone who has always handled the household bills starts struggling with them, mention it to a doctor: the National Institute on Aging lists trouble handling money and paying bills among the signs of mild Alzheimer's disease, and a doctor can look into the cause ([NIA](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-symptoms-and-diagnosis/what-are-signs-alzheimers-disease)).
