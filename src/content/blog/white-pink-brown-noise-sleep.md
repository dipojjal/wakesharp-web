The apartment above yours has hardwood floors and a tenant who does their best thinking while pacing. A bus exhales at the stop outside. The refrigerator clicks on, then off, then on again. You have been horizontal for forty minutes, you gave up around minute twenty-five and reached for your phone, and now you are scrolling a wall of sound options: white, pink, brown, grey, green, "deep ocean rumble," "1974 window air conditioner." Every one of them has five stars and a review claiming it cured somebody's insomnia.

Here is the short version before you pick one. **The color is mostly preference. Masking is the actual mechanism. And the evidence that any of this reliably improves sleep is thinner than the marketing implies.** That is not a reason to skip it. It is a reason to try it cheaply, judge it honestly, and keep your expectations small.

## The question is not "which color," it is "what is the sound for"

A quiet room is not actually the goal. A *predictable* room is. Your auditory system keeps working while you sleep, and it is tuned to change rather than to absolute volume. A steady 45 dB hum is boring. A 45 dB hum with a car door slamming through it is an event, and events are what pull you up out of deep sleep.

That distinction has been measured. In a lab study where healthy sleepers were exposed to recorded intensive care unit noise, [Stanchina and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2004.12.004) found that arousals dropped from roughly 48 per hour under ICU noise alone to about 16 per hour when broadband noise was added to the room. The interesting part is why. The size of the jump from background level to peak level, not the peak level itself, predicted whether a sleeper woke. Raising the floor shrank the jump.

So a sound machine is not sedating you. It is not "calming your nervous system." It is filling in the valleys between sounds so the peaks stop sticking out. Everything else you have read about noise colors is downstream of that one job.

## What the evidence actually says, including the parts nobody quotes

This is the part where a product blog would cite one small trial and move on. The honest picture is messier.

The most thorough look at this is a systematic review by [Riedy, Smith, Rocha and Basner](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101385) in *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, which pulled together 38 studies of continuous broadband noise and sleep. Their conclusion was blunt: the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep is of very low quality, the studies are small and inconsistent, and some of them found continuous noise made sleep worse rather than better. They also pointed out that a fair number of studies were not designed in a way that could separate a real effect from expectation.

A second review, [Capezuti and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.9860) in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, reached a similar place from a different angle. No strong evidence of benefit across the auditory stimulation literature, but also no reported adverse effects from short-term use during sleep. In the single study they found that compared white noise directly with pink noise, the white noise group showed no improvement and the pink noise results were mixed. That is the closest thing to a head-to-head answer on **brown noise vs white noise** that the literature offers, and it is barely an answer at all.

The most commonly cited positive result is worth reading carefully too. [Ebben, Yan and Krieger](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2021.03.031) gave white noise machines to New Yorkers with noisy bedrooms and found less time awake after falling asleep and shorter self-reported sleep latency. Encouraging, and also: ten participants, no blinding, and the improvement persisted during the washout week after the machines were taken away. The authors themselves noted that persistence points to something other than direct acoustic masking, which is a polite way of saying expectation may be doing real work here.

None of this means sound machines are useless. It means the correct summary is "plausible mechanism, weak evidence, low risk, high individual variation." If someone tells you the science is settled, they are selling a speaker.

## White, pink, brown: what the words actually mean

The colors are not moods. They describe how sound energy is distributed across frequency, and the differences are simple.

- **White noise** has roughly equal power per unit of frequency. Because human hearing groups high frequencies into wider bands, white noise sounds bright and hissy, like an untuned radio or a shower running.
- **Pink noise** falls off at about 1/f, meaning power drops as frequency rises. Equal power per octave. It sounds balanced and full, closer to steady rain or wind through trees.
- **Brown noise** (also called red or Brownian noise) falls off faster still, around 1/f squared. Nearly all the energy sits low. It sounds like a distant waterfall, a jet cabin, or a large truck idling two streets away.

Brown noise sounds "deeper" for exactly one reason: the treble has been rolled off. That is the entire difference. Its recent popularity is a genuine preference signal (a lot of people find hiss irritating and rumble soothing) but it is not evidence of superior sleep physiology.

One thing worth separating out, because it gets mangled constantly: there is real, careful research on pink noise and deep sleep, and it is not about running a sound machine all night. [Papalambros and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00109) at Northwestern delivered *short bursts* of pink noise timed precisely to the upstate of slow oscillations in sleeping older adults, and saw enhanced slow wave activity along with better overnight word recall. That requires EEG and phase-locked timing. A continuous pink noise track on your phone is not doing that, and no one should imply otherwise.

**Verdict: pick the one you find least annoying.** Genuinely. The masking effect depends on covering the frequencies of whatever is bothering you, and preference determines whether you relax or lie there resenting the noise. If your problem is traffic and low rumble, a bass-heavy brown track may just add to the mud, and something with more midrange may mask better. If your problem is voices or a television through a wall, midrange coverage matters more than depth.

## Seven things to get right if you are trying this tonight

1. **Set a volume ceiling and stay under it.** The rough target is quiet conversation level, loud enough to blur the edges of intrusions and no louder. If you cannot hear yourself say a sentence normally over it, it is too loud. Louder is not better masking; it is just more sound in your night.
2. **Take the hearing-safety point seriously, especially for children.** When researchers measured 14 infant sleep machines at maximum volume, [all of them exceeded 50 dBA at 30 cm](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3617) and three exceeded 85 dBA, a level that over an eight-hour night sits above adult occupational exposure limits. Keep any machine well away from a crib, and do not run it at maximum.
3. **Put it between you and the noise, not next to your head.** Placement near the door or window does more masking per decibel than a speaker on the nightstand, and it keeps peak exposure at your ear lower.
4. **Use a continuous source, not a short loop.** A three-minute file with an audible seam every three minutes reintroduces exactly the kind of predictable change your brain is good at detecting. Long or generated tracks are worth the extra download.
5. **Decide deliberately whether to run it all night.** All night is the point if your disturbances come at 3 AM (garbage trucks, a partner's alarm, early traffic). If your only problem is falling asleep, a timed fade after 45 minutes gets you the benefit without an eight-hour sound bath.
6. **Give it two weeks, then actually judge it.** Track one thing, like how often you woke, not a vague sense of sleep quality. If nothing changes, turn it off. The evidence base does not justify loyalty to a device that is not working for you.
7. **Fix the room first where you can.** Masking is a workaround for noise you cannot remove. A door sweep, a rug, moving the bed away from a shared wall, or earplugs will often beat any speaker, and cost less.

## If masking is not your problem

Noise is one of several reasons a bedroom refuses to cooperate, and a sound machine cannot fix the others. If you are lying awake with a busy head rather than a busy street, the useful lever is usually the hour before bed rather than the sound during it. ## The morning-side catch nobody mentions

Here is the trade you are making, stated plainly. A sound machine works by raising your arousal threshold. Your alarm's entire job is to cross that threshold. Running masking noise all night makes the morning slightly harder by design.

That is usually fine, and it is worth two checks. First, make sure your alarm is genuinely audible over your masking sound, ideally by testing it once while the machine is running rather than discovering the answer on a Monday. Second, be aware that a threshold-raised, half-asleep brain is very good at silencing a sound it barely registered. That is the failure mode behind [alarms you swear never rang](/blog/sleep-through-alarm-dismissed-no-memory).

## FAQ

### Is brown noise better than white noise for sleep?

There is no good evidence that it is. The two reviews that have looked hardest at this found the overall evidence weak, and the one direct white versus pink comparison they identified produced no clear winner. Brown noise sounds deeper because its energy falls off steeply with frequency, which many people prefer, and preference is a legitimate reason to choose it. It is just not a physiological advantage.

### Should I run white noise all night or use a sleep timer?

It depends on when your disturbances happen. If noise wakes you in the small hours, a timer defeats the purpose and continuous is better. If you only struggle to fall asleep, a 30 to 60 minute fade gives you the same help with less total exposure. Try one for a fortnight, then the other.

### How loud should a sound machine be?

Quiet. Aim for something close to a soft conversation, positioned between you and the noise source rather than beside your head. Turning it up does not improve masking proportionally, and sustained high output carries real hearing risk. Measured infant sleep machines have hit levels above adult occupational limits at maximum volume, so treat the top of the dial as decoration.

### Will white noise make me sleep through my alarm?

It can make your alarm harder to hear, which amounts to the same thing. Masking raises the arousal threshold your alarm has to cross. Test your alarm once with the machine running at its usual volume, keep the phone or clock on the far side of the room from the speaker, and if you regularly wake with no memory of dismissing anything, treat that as a signal to change the setup rather than the sound.
