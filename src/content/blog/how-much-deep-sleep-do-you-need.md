It is 6:38 AM, and before your feet have found the floor you have opened the sleep app. Time asleep: 7 hr 12 min. Respectable. Then the stages chart, and its thin stripe near the start of the night: Deep, 34 min. On Sunday it said 1 hr 3 min.

Ninety seconds ago you felt fine. Now you feel like a person who got 34 minutes of deep sleep, and you are not sure whether that is a health problem, a measurement problem or both. So you search, and the internet answers with great confidence and no agreement: an hour, two hours, a fifth of the night, a quarter of it.

**The short answer:** there is no official target for deep sleep. Most adults spend about 10 to 20 percent of the night in it, roughly 40 to 110 minutes across seven to nine hours of sleep, with more in your teens and twenties, less from midlife on, and usually less in men than in women. REM takes about another 20 to 25 percent. The target that does exist is for total sleep, 7 or more hours for adults, and deep sleep is the part your brain takes first.

## What deep sleep is, and what it does

Each night you cycle through light sleep, deep sleep and REM. Deep sleep is non-REM stage 3, or N3, also called slow-wave sleep after the slow brain waves that define it on a recording. Most of it comes early in the night, and most REM later ([NHLBI](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/stages-of-sleep)); [our piece on the 90-minute myth](/blog/sleep-cycles-90-minute-myth) covers how those cycles stack up.

What it does, on evidence of varying strength:

- **Hormones.** In 149 healthy men aged 16 to 83, growth hormone release tracked the amount of deep sleep regardless of age ([Van Cauter et al., JAMA, 2000](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/192981)).
- **Blood sugar.** Suppressing young, healthy adults' deep sleep all night, without shortening their sleep, markedly reduced their insulin sensitivity and, with it, their glucose tolerance ([Tasali et al., PNAS, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18172212/)).
- **Memory.** Sounds played in step with the brain's slow waves strengthened them and improved the consolidation of what people had learned before sleep ([Ngo et al., Neuron, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23583623/)).
- **Waste clearance, unsettled.** The idea that sleep rinses waste from the brain rests mostly on mice: a 2013 study saw the space between brain cells grow by 60 percent during sleep ([Xie et al., Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24136970/)), and a 2024 study found clearance markedly reduced during sleep instead ([Miao et al., Nature Neuroscience](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38741022/)).

## How much deep sleep do you need?

There is no agreed nightly number. When a National Sleep Foundation panel set out markers of good sleep quality, it agreed on continuity measures, such as time to fall asleep and time awake in the night, but found less or no consensus on sleep stages ([Ohayon et al., Sleep Health, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28346153/)). Descriptively, the Sleep Foundation puts the typical adult share at 10 to 20 percent of the night, roughly 40 to 110 minutes on seven to nine hours of sleep ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/stages-of-sleep/deep-sleep)). In a 2024 lab study, healthy adults aged 20 to 50 averaged about 95 minutes ([Robbins et al., Sensors, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11511193/)).

Age moves the number more than anything you will do. Slow-wave sleep peaks in early childhood, drops sharply in the teens and keeps declining through adulthood; some older people have none at all (NHLBI). In Van Cauter's men, it fell from 18.9 percent of sleep at ages 16 to 25 to 3.4 percent at 36 to 50, then barely changed. Among 2,685 adults aged 37 to 92, women had roughly twice as much as men ([Redline et al., Archives of Internal Medicine, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14980992/)). Exact figures swing from lab to lab; the direction does not.

Two things follow. A 45-year-old man comparing his deep sleep with his own at 25 is using the wrong ruler. And deep sleep is the stage your brain protects. When nine young volunteers were held to four hours of sleep a night for four nights, their slow-wave sleep was largely unaffected while light sleep and REM were cut, and it rose on the first recovery night ([Brunner et al., Sleep, 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8446828/)). A short night mostly costs you the other stages.

## How much REM sleep do you need?

REM, the stage where most dreaming happens, usually makes up 20 to 25 percent of the night (Sleep Foundation), and it has no official target either. The practical difference between deep sleep and REM sleep is timing: deep sleep is front-loaded and REM back-loaded. Cut a night short at the end and you lose REM far more than deep sleep, so the alarm you set 45 minutes early for the gym comes mostly out of REM.

## Are sleep trackers accurate for deep sleep?

Roughly, at best. Deep sleep is defined by brain waves, and no watch or ring reads them. Apple's own validation paper, updated in October 2025, says Apple Watch estimates stages from accelerometer signals, including the small movements of breathing, and that its Core label covers stages N1 and N2, what some trackers call light sleep. Against lab-scored nights, the current algorithm labeled 68 percent of true deep sleep as Deep and nearly all the rest as Core ([Apple](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Estimating_Sleep_Stages_from_Apple_Watch_Oct_2025.pdf)).

In the 2024 Robbins study, 35 healthy adults spent a night in a sleep lab wearing an Oura Ring, a Fitbit Sense 2 and an Apple Watch Series 8. All three were good at detecting sleep. For deep sleep, the Oura did not differ significantly from the lab on average, the Fitbit undercounted by 15 minutes, and the Apple Watch undercounted by 43 while overcounting light sleep by 45. Fine print: Oura funded the study, the first author sits on Oura's medical advisory board, and the watch was tested before Apple's 2025 update. Still, on those averages one night could read as about 50 minutes of deep sleep on a wrist and an hour and a half on a finger.

So one night's figure deserves very little weight, and a trend over weeks only a little more. The bigger risk is the number becoming the problem: researchers call a perfectionistic quest for ideal sleep, driven by tracker data, orthosomnia ([Baron et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855740/)).

## Why waking up from deep sleep feels worst

Deep sleep has one effect you can feel. Pulled out of it, you can be foggy for up to an hour, the state called sleep inertia (Sleep Foundation). Since deep sleep crowds into the early night, the classic victim is someone woken a couple of hours after lights out by a phone, a smoke alarm or a child; a morning alarm after a full night more often lands in lighter sleep or REM. Timing an alarm to dodge it is mostly guesswork, as the cycles piece above explains.

## How to get more deep sleep naturally

The effects are modest, but these levers have evidence behind them.

### 1. Protect the total, and keep your wake time steady

Deep sleep comes first, so protecting the total protects it, along with the REM a short night takes. Adults should get 7 or more hours ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)), and the Sleep Foundation's advice for more deep sleep includes a consistent schedule. Our [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) works backward from your wake time in hours, not cycles.

### 2. Keep naps early and short

Deep sleep is driven by sleep pressure, which builds the longer you are awake. Spend some of it late in the day and the night gets less: in healthy young men, an early-evening nap blunted the rise of slow-wave activity through the first three stretches of non-REM sleep that night ([Werth et al., American Journal of Physiology, 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8853369/)). If you nap, earlier and shorter leaves more for the night.

### 3. Move during the day

In a meta-analysis, a single bout of exercise brought small increases in slow-wave sleep and total sleep and a moderate drop in time awake at night, varying with factors such as timing and duration ([Kredlow et al., Journal of Behavioral Medicine, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25596964/)). Small is the honest word, but it pushes the right way.

### 4. Move your caffeine cutoff earlier

A 2023 meta-analysis found caffeine cut about 11 minutes of deep sleep and 45 of total sleep, and put the cutoff for a 250 mL coffee at least 8.8 hours before bed ([Gardiner et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36870101/)). [Our caffeine cutoff piece](/blog/caffeine-cutoff-time-sleep) does the arithmetic for your own cup.

### 5. Don't let the bedroom run hot

With normal bedding and clothes, heat increases wakefulness and reduces slow-wave and REM sleep, while cold exposure did not change sleep stages ([Okamoto-Mizuno and Mizuno, Journal of Physiological Anthropology, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22738673/)). Err on the cool side and add a blanket.

### 6. Don't count alcohol as a sleep aid

Most studies find alcohol increases deep sleep in the first half of the night, then disrupts the second half ([Ebrahim et al., Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)), so a tracker may even show a healthy deep sleep figure after a few drinks. [Our piece on alcohol and sleep architecture](/blog/alcohol-sleep-architecture) covers what that bargain really costs.

### What doesn't work

Sound is where the hype outruns the research. In the lab, sounds boosted slow waves only when each was timed to the sleeper's own waves as they happened; the same sounds out of step did nothing (Ngo et al.). A playlist cannot see your brain waves, and staring at last night's graph does nothing for tonight's.

## Where WakeSharp fits, and where it doesn't

[WakeSharp](/download), an alarm app for iPhone and Android, has no sleep tracking. It does not know when you fell asleep, how much deep sleep you got or which stage your alarm interrupted, so it makes no attempt to time the alarm to a lighter moment.

What it gives you is a separate check on how the morning goes. The alarm asks for a mission, such as Math Problems or Memory Match, and the missions you finish feed a daily [Sharpness Score](/features/sharpness-score), kept in the app with badges and a history of your mornings. Read that history beside your tracker and a one-line note on how you feel; if your deep sleep figure lurches from night to night while your mornings stay much the same, hold the figure loosely.

The Sharpness Score is not a sleep score, a cognitive test or a medical assessment, and it does not tell you whether you are safe to drive or work. One morning's number means little, and a change in it does not prove what caused it.

## FAQ

### How many hours of deep sleep should you get?

There is no official number. Adults are advised to get 7 or more hours of total sleep, and a National Sleep Foundation expert panel did not settle on stage amounts as markers of good sleep. As a description rather than a prescription, 40 to 110 minutes covers most adults on seven to nine hours of sleep, and less is normal from midlife on, especially in men.

### How much sleep do you need by age?

The CDC recommends 9 to 12 hours for children aged 6 to 12, 8 to 10 for teens, 7 or more for adults aged 18 to 60, 7 to 9 from 61 to 64, and 7 to 8 from 65 on; younger children need more, naps included. Deep sleep declines with age even where those hours barely change, so your total is the better yardstick.

### Is 30 minutes of deep sleep enough?

It can be, and the figure may be wrong anyway. Wrist trackers tend to file some deep sleep as light: in a 2024 lab comparison, an Apple Watch Series 8 undercounted it by 43 minutes on average, and Apple's own testing has its current algorithm labeling about a third of true deep sleep as Core. If you regularly sleep seven or more hours and still wake unrefreshed, talk to a clinician rather than buying a better tracker.

### Why do I need 10 hours of sleep to feel rested?

Sometimes it is repayment: in a lab study, volunteers held to four hours a night for four nights got extra slow-wave sleep on their first recovery night, so an occasional long night after a short week is not unusual. If you regularly need 10 or more hours and still feel tired, raise it with a clinician. Cleveland Clinic lists too little sleep, poor-quality sleep (including from sleep apnea), depression and some medications among the causes of excessive sleepiness, and advises seeing a provider if you feel tired all the time or can't control when you fall asleep ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21591-hypersomnia)).

## The number worth chasing

Deep sleep is the stage your brain protects, which makes it a bad thing to micromanage and an easy thing to support: enough hours, a steady wake time, an earlier last coffee, a cooler room, the nightcap left out.

The 34 minutes on your screen this morning was an estimate. The 7 hours and 12 minutes was the part you controlled. Spend your attention there, and on how the next few mornings actually go.
