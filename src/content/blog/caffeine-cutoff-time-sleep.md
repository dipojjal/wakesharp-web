It is 9:40 in the evening and you are doing everything right. Lamps low, phone charging on the dresser, a paperback instead of a screen. And you are wide awake in that particular way where your body is heavy but your head keeps opening new tabs.

Rewind eleven hours. Coffee at 8 AM, another around 11, and then one more at 3:20 because a meeting overran and the alternative was slumping through it. That third cup is the one still clocked in.

The reflex here is to file the problem under character. You have bad sleep hygiene, you lack discipline, you should just be more careful. But there is nothing in that story you can act on. What you are actually dealing with is a drug with a long, boring, entirely predictable elimination curve, and a bit of arithmetic that almost nobody does.

## Caffeine does not add energy. It hides the tiredness.

While you are awake, a molecule called adenosine accumulates in the brain and pushes on receptors that make you feel sleepy. That accumulation is one of the two main forces deciding when you fall asleep and how deeply.

Caffeine works by getting there first. It antagonises all four adenosine receptor subtypes, and the A2A subtype in particular is tied to wakefulness ([StatPearls, NIH Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519490/)). It parks in the spaces adenosine wants. The adenosine keeps piling up regardless; you simply stop receiving the notification.

This is why caffeine feels like a loan rather than income. It does not reduce your sleep pressure. It mutes your awareness of it, and the muting wears off on a schedule.

### The half-life, and the half after that

In a healthy adult, caffeine's half-life averages roughly five hours, with a normal spread of about three to seven. Run the doubling backwards on a single 200 mg cup at 3 PM:

- 8 PM: about 100 mg still circulating
- 1 AM: about 50 mg
- 6 AM: about 25 mg

Fifty milligrams at 1 AM is not a rounding error. That is roughly a can of cola's worth of an alertness drug sitting in your bloodstream during the stretch of night where your deepest sleep is supposed to happen. The tail is not ten hours of full-strength stimulation. It is ten hours of a slowly shrinking amount that never quite reaches zero before your alarm does.

### The disruption you are least likely to notice

The most useful study here is small and unusually clean. Drake and colleagues gave twelve healthy adults 400 mg of caffeine at bedtime, three hours before bed, or six hours before bed, in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover ([Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)). All three timings cost more than an hour of total sleep compared with placebo.

The finding that should change your behaviour is not the hour. It is this: at bedtime and at three hours, participants' own sleep diaries picked up the damage. At six hours, only the objective monitor did. People genuinely could not feel a night that had been measurably shredded.

That is the trap. You fall asleep on time, you rate the night as fine, and you wake up flat and blame the weather, your age, your mattress, or your character. Meanwhile the EEG evidence has been consistent for decades that caffeine suppresses slow-wave activity, the low-frequency signature of deep sleep ([Landolt et al., Neuropsychopharmacology, 1995](https://www.nature.com/articles/1380255)). In a companion study, 200 mg given at 07:10 in the morning still measurably reduced sleep efficiency and total sleep time in nine healthy men that same night ([Brain Research, 1995](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/000689939500040W)). Morning coffee. Same night's sleep.

There is a clock effect too. A double-espresso dose taken three hours before habitual bedtime delayed the circadian melatonin rhythm by around 40 minutes, close to half the delay produced by three hours of bright evening light ([Burke et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2015](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.aac5125)). So evening caffeine both fragments the night and nudges tomorrow later, which is a decent explanation for the specific fog described in [our piece on sleep inertia](/blog/sleep-inertia-why-you-wake-up-groggy).

## So how long is the tail, actually

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 24 studies and put numbers on it: caffeine cut total sleep time by about 45 minutes, cut sleep efficiency by 7 percent, added 9 minutes to falling asleep and 12 minutes of wakefulness once asleep, increased light sleep and shrank the share of deep sleep ([Gardiner et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36870101/)).

They also modelled cutoffs. To protect total sleep time, a 250 mL coffee at 107 mg should be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed, and a standard pre-workout serving at 217.5 mg at least 13.2 hours before bed. Black tea, at typical strength, did not produce a cutoff at all.

Look hard at those doses. A 107 mg coffee is a modest home cup. Large takeaway drinks routinely carry two to three times that, which is how "at least nine hours" becomes ten or more. The old rule about no coffee after 2 PM turns out to be roughly what the arithmetic spits out for an 11 PM bedtime and a real-world cup.

## Five things worth doing about it

### 1. Calculate your own cutoff rather than borrowing a number

Start from your target bedtime and subtract: about 9 hours for a standard cup, 10 to 12 for a large one or a double shot, and 13 for a pre-workout or a high-caffeine energy drink. An 11 PM bedtime with an ordinary coffee lands your last cup at 2 PM. Treat that as a hypothesis you are going to test, not a commandment.

### 2. Move the timing before you cut the dose

Most people trying to fix caffeine start by drinking less, which makes the afternoon worse without reliably making the night better. Timing is the cheaper lever. Keep your daily total the same and front-load it. You lose almost nothing you actually valued, because the cup you enjoy most is rarely the 3:20 one you drank out of desperation.

### 3. Give the afternoon dip what it is actually asking for

The mid-afternoon flatness is a normal feature of the daily rhythm, not proof of a caffeine deficiency. Ten minutes of outdoor daylight, a short walk, a glass of water, or a genuinely short nap earlier in the afternoon all address it without spending your night. None of these feel as decisive as a coffee. That is precisely why the coffee wins, and why deciding in advance beats deciding at 3:20.

### 4. Decaf is not zero, but it is close

Analysis of ten decaffeinated coffees bought from different outlets found 0 to 13.9 mg of caffeine per 16-ounce serving, with repeat samples from a single outlet clustering between 12.0 and 13.4 mg ([McCusker et al., Journal of Analytical Toxicology, 2006](https://academic.oup.com/jat/article/30/8/611/714415)). Against a 200 mg afternoon cup, an evening decaf is noise for most people. It is worth knowing in both directions: usually fine, and not literally nothing if you are unusually sensitive.

### 5. Check which personal multipliers apply to you

Caffeine is cleared almost entirely by one liver enzyme, CYP1A2, and clearance varies enormously between people. A comprehensive review lists age, sex and hormones, liver disease, obesity, smoking and diet among the factors that shift it ([Nehlig, Pharmacological Reviews, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29514871/)). Pregnancy, especially the third trimester, can stretch the half-life to around 15 hours. For general intake, the FDA cites 400 mg a day as a reasonable ceiling for most healthy adults while noting wide variation in both sensitivity and elimination speed ([FDA consumer update](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)).

If you are pregnant or nursing, taking medication, or managing a heart rhythm or anxiety condition, this is a conversation for your clinician rather than a blog post. Caffeine interacts with more prescriptions than people expect.

## Run the seven-day version on yourself

Here is the experiment, and it is deliberately small.

For one week, write down two things each day: the clock time of your last caffeine, and a rough milligram estimate. Then record one number the next morning, the same way every day, before the day contaminates it.

This is where WakeSharp's warm-up games happen to be useful. They run after you have dismissed the alarm and produce a Sharpness Score measured against your own baseline rather than against strangers on a leaderboard. Line up seven last-caffeine times against seven morning scores and you have something closer to a personal dose-timing curve than any general rule can give you.

Be honest about what that is worth. WakeSharp does not track your sleep and does not try to. A Sharpness Score reflects how you did on a short game a minute after waking, which is also shaped by your bedtime, your stress, a noisy street, and plain randomness. Seven data points is a hint, not a result. But if your two latest-caffeine days are also your two flattest mornings, that is a pattern worth acting on, and if the line is flat you may simply be a fast metaboliser. Either answer is more useful than guessing.

One thing the experiment will not fix on its own: if you are staying up late because the evening is the only time that belongs to you, moving your coffee earlier just means lying awake with better pharmacology. That is a different problem, and we wrote about it in [revenge bedtime procrastination](/blog/revenge-bedtime-procrastination).

## FAQ

### How many hours before bed should I stop drinking caffeine?

For a standard coffee, the meta-analytic estimate is at least 8.8 hours before bedtime to protect total sleep time, which rounds to about nine. For a large drink, a double shot, or a pre-workout supplement, push that to 10 to 13 hours. If you go to bed at 11 PM, a 1 to 2 PM cutoff is a sensible starting point, then adjust based on a week of your own data.

### Does caffeine still hurt my sleep if I fall asleep easily?

Yes, and that is the uncomfortable part. In the Drake study, caffeine taken six hours before bed cost over an hour of sleep, but participants' own diaries did not detect it. Falling asleep quickly tells you about sleep onset. It tells you very little about how fragmented or how shallow the rest of the night was.

### Is decaf coffee okay in the evening?

For most people, yes. Measured decaffeinated coffees came in between 0 and about 14 mg per large serving, a small fraction of a regular cup. If you are highly caffeine-sensitive or a slow metaboliser, even that may be worth testing rather than assuming.

### Does tolerance mean caffeine stops affecting my sleep?

Tolerance to the subjective jolt develops faster than protection for your sleep. Habitual drinkers often stop feeling the buzz while still showing measurable changes in sleep architecture. Feeling nothing from a cup is not evidence that it is doing nothing at 2 AM.

## The evening version of this

If it is already late and you had that 3:20 coffee, tonight is mostly a write-off in terms of prevention. There is no way to speed elimination up, whatever the internet says about water and cold showers. What you can do is not compound it: keep the lights low, accept that sleep may take a while, and resist the urge to fix a caffeine problem with an alcohol solution, which trades sleep onset for a worse second half of the night.

Then make one decision for tomorrow, while it is still fresh and slightly annoying. Pick your cutoff time, write it somewhere you will see it at 3 PM, and start counting. The tail is long, but it is at least predictable, which makes it one of the few things about your sleep you can move on purpose.
