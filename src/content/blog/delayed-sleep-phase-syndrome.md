It is 3:05 on a Thursday morning, and you are not anxious, not wired, not scrolling. You are simply awake, the way other people are awake at 3 in the afternoon. You went to bed at 11 because the alarm is set for 6:30, and every night this week sleep has arrived between 2:30 and 3:15: late, but punctual, like a train running three hours behind to the minute.

On Saturday, with no alarm, you will fall asleep at 3 without a fight, wake at 11 and feel fine. Eight solid hours, nothing wrong with them except the clock time.

It has been like this since you were about fifteen. Early nights have been tried, and so has willpower. If every attempt to move earlier snaps back by the weekend, this may be more than being a night owl.

**The short answer:** delayed sleep phase syndrome (DSPS), now formally called delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, is a body clock that runs two or more hours later than the schedule you need, so sleep will not come until the small hours and a 7 AM alarm lands in your biological night. Left to your own hours, you sleep normally. It is most common in teens and young adults, and treatment is all about timing: a steady wake time, bright light soon after waking, and melatonin timed by a clinician.

## What is delayed sleep phase syndrome?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine defines it as a sleep pattern delayed by two or more hours from societal norms, because the internal clock runs later at night and later in the morning ([AASM Sleep Education](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/delayed-sleep-wake-phase/)). Once sleep starts it is generally normal, and on your own schedule you get a normal amount. It becomes a disorder when that timing collides with work or school.

The signs the AASM lists:

- Sleep that runs later than the times you want or need
- Trouble falling asleep at your target bedtime
- Great difficulty waking in the morning
- Normal sleep when nothing forces the timing
- A late but stable pattern, not one that drifts

A sleep diary shows the giveaway: short sleep in the week, long sleep at the weekend. At night DSPS looks like insomnia (the AASM estimates it turns up in about 10 percent of people who complain of insomnia); in the morning it looks like laziness. A Flinders University review calls it the most commonly diagnosed body clock disorder ([Micic et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26434674/)).

## Night owl or delayed sleep phase syndrome: what's the difference?

A night owl is a chronotype, a place on a normal spectrum, not a diagnosis, though the AASM lists being an evening type as a risk factor for DSPS. The difference is persistence and damage. The Flinders review describes DSPS as a persistent inability to fall asleep at conventional times, with extreme difficulty waking, and Cleveland Clinic notes that night owls do not have the severe daytime sleepiness DSPS brings ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/14295-delayed-sleep-phase-syndrome-dsps)).

A practical test is to try [an owl's plan](/blog/night-owl-wake-up-early) first: a fixed wake time, morning light and dim evenings. If your sleep moves and the change holds, you were probably dealing with a late chronotype. If sleep still will not come until the small hours, or everything collapses the first weekend it is allowed to, take the pattern to a clinician.

That visit matters for another reason. When researchers measured the body clocks of 26 people diagnosed with the disorder, roughly half had normally timed clocks; the authors suggest a mismatch between clock and sleep, or learned trouble falling asleep ([Micic et al., Sleep, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32619243/)). Same symptoms, different treatment.

## Why the body clock runs late

Your body clock runs a little longer than 24 hours on average, and light pulls it back into line each day. Timing sets the direction: light late in your biological day and early in your biological night pushes the clock later, light late in the biological night and early morning pulls it earlier, and ordinary indoor light is enough to register ([Duffy and Czeisler, Sleep Medicine Clinics, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2717723/)).

DSPS tilts that system in several ways:

- **A longer internal day.** In a small lab study, six people with the disorder had a body temperature cycle of 24 hours 54 minutes, against 24 hours 29 minutes in good sleepers, one reason patients struggle to move earlier and relapse often ([Micic et al., Journal of Sleep Research, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23899423/)).
- **Extra sensitivity to light at night.** After six and a half hours of light at about 150 lux, an ordinary indoor level, ten men with the disorder shifted later by 31.5 percent more than eleven controls, once timing was accounted for ([Watson et al., Journal of Physiology, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30281150/)). The evening that nudges a housemate's clock shoves yours.
- **Genes.** About 40 percent have a family history, per the AASM, and one hereditary form traces to a variant of the clock gene CRY1 that lengthens the clock's cycle ([Patke et al., Cell, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28388406/)).
- **Age.** Sleep timing shifts later through the teens, and DSPS, which typically starts then, may be an extreme version of that shift ([Crowley, Acebo and Carskadon, Sleep Medicine, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17383934/)).

The AASM estimates 7 to 16 percent of teens and young adults may have it, though definitions move the number: a survey of 10,220 Norwegian 16- to 18-year-olds found 3.3 percent, and more than half of them also met criteria for insomnia ([Sivertsen et al., BMC Public Health, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3878844/)).

## How do you fix delayed sleep phase syndrome?

Cleveland Clinic is frank that there is no cure, only ongoing management, and the Flinders review notes frequent relapse after treatments that work in the short term. Resetting a late circadian rhythm takes timed steps that support each other.

### 1. Get measured first

Start a sleep diary tonight: bedtime, roughly when you fell asleep, when you woke, and whether an alarm did it. The AASM's 2015 guideline asks for symptoms lasting at least three months, a diary or wrist actigraphy covering at least seven days, and a melatonin-onset test if feasible ([Auger et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4582061/)). Cleveland Clinic notes DSPS can be hard to tell from narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia, which a clinician will rule out.

### 2. Hold one wake time, seven days a week

Every other lever is timed against it. Both relapses at six months in the adolescent trial below were put down to late nights and lie-ins on weekends and holidays. A fixed schedule alone is not a proven treatment (the AASM found insufficient evidence for it), but nothing holds without it. Pick a time you can keep on Saturday and let the plan move it; forcing an early start just cuts your sleep short.

### 3. Bright light after waking, moved earlier in steps

Light pushes the clock later if it lands before your core body temperature low point and earlier if it lands after (AASM guideline), and that low point typically falls two to three hours before your natural wake time ([Bartlett, Biggs and Armstrong, Medical Journal of Australia, 2013](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2013/199/8/circadian-rhythm-disorders-among-adolescents-assessment-and-treatment-options)). If your body would wake at 11, a lamp at 6 AM can land on the wrong side and push you later.

So treatment starts where you are. In a randomised trial of 49 adolescents, light began at each teen's natural wake time and moved 30 minutes earlier each day, using sunlight or a lamp, alongside cognitive behavioural therapy. Afterwards 13 percent still met criteria for the disorder, against 82 percent of a waitlist group ([Gradisar et al., Sleep, 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)).

In adults the evidence is thinner: the AASM found too little to recommend light alone, and in a 2021 systematic review, benefits faded in the two small studies that followed people up ([Gomes et al., Sleep Science, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8340891/)). If you use a lamp, our [SAD lamp guide](/blog/sad-lamp-light-therapy-guide) covers distance, timing and safety.

### 4. Dim the evenings

If light at night pushes your clock harder than other people's, the last hours before bed are where you lose ground, so lower the lights and keep bright screens away from your face. Avoiding light has little trial evidence on its own, but in an early study of 20 patients, dim evenings with two hours of bright morning light moved temperature rhythms earlier ([Rosenthal et al., Sleep, 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2267478/)).

### 5. Melatonin, timed by a clinician

The AASM guideline suggests strategically timed melatonin for adults and young people with the disorder, a recommendation it grades as weak. In a double-blind trial of 116 people, melatonin with a scheduled bedtime brought sleep onset 34 minutes earlier than placebo with the same schedule, and clinicians rated 53 percent of the melatonin group much or very much improved, against 24 percent on placebo ([Sletten et al., PLOS Medicine, 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1002587)). Timed is the key word: at the right point before your own evening melatonin rise it works as a clock signal, not a sleeping pill, and that point differs between people. The AASM says dose and timing belong with a sleep doctor; our [melatonin explainer](/blog/melatonin-less-is-more) covers why more is not better.

### 6. Skip do-it-yourself chronotherapy

Older advice suggests chronotherapy: pushing bedtime a few hours later each night until it comes round the clock. The AASM set aside a 1981 chronotherapy study because it had only five subjects, and notes a report of an adult whose clock stopped holding a 24-hour day afterward. Not a weekend project.

## What an alarm can and can't do here

The piece an alarm can hold is the wake time, every day, at your plan's current step. [WakeSharp](/download) is an alarm app for iPhone and Android where the alarm ends with a mission you choose. [First Light](/features/walking-alarm-clock) asks you to walk to a window and hold your phone in the light. Morning light is the main cue the body clock gets that the day has started, and because a DSPS plan starts from your current late wake time, its early steps often fall after sunrise. As the steps reach December darkness, switch to Walk It Off, which counts real steps, and end the walk at your lamp. However you quiet the alarm, the morning only counts once the mission is done.

Set the alarm to the step you are on, not the time you are aiming for: a 6 AM alarm on day one of a plan that starts at 10:30 is the wrong-side-of-the-low-point mistake with a ringtone.

The limits: WakeSharp is not a treatment. It has no sleep tracking, it cannot tell where your clock is, and a mission at a window is not a measured dose of light. Your phone's own controls always work: nothing stops you switching the phone off. No alarm can guarantee you wake, so for anything you cannot miss, set a backup on a second device.

## FAQ

### Does melatonin help delayed sleep phase syndrome?

Often, when it is timed well. DSPS is one of the places melatonin has real support, with a recommendation in the AASM guideline and a 2018 trial in which sleep onset came 34 minutes earlier than with placebo. Taken at your current late bedtime like a sleeping pill, it is aimed at the wrong problem. Get the dose and timing from a sleep clinician, especially for a teenager or alongside other medication.

### Why do I sleep in so late?

Often because the week did not let you sleep when your body wanted to. With a late clock, weekday alarms cut sleep short and the weekend collects the debt, the classic DSPS diary. Late nights, ordinary sleep debt and some medications do the same. If you stay sleepy even on your own schedule, that is not DSPS behaving normally, and it deserves a clinician's look.

### Can humans be nocturnal?

Humans are a daytime species, and someone with DSPS is not nocturnal: their clock still runs on a roughly 24-hour day, just a few hours late. For people who do not respond to treatment, some clinicians argue for accommodating the clock instead, with flexible hours and support where strict schedules cannot be met, as the AASM guideline summarises. Building your life around sleeping from 3 to 11 is a legitimate answer, not a failure.

## Start the diary tonight

If the 3:05 scene is your week, keep the diary for two weeks, hold a wake time you can actually keep, and ask your doctor or a sleep clinic whether this could be delayed sleep-wake phase disorder. The answer decides the treatment.

Go sooner if you are sleepy even after a week on your own hours, if your sleep drifts later every day instead of settling (which can point to a different disorder, non-24-hour rhythm), if you are leaning on alcohol or sedatives to sleep, or if low mood has come with it (depression often travels with DSPS). If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US.

A clock that runs late is not a character flaw. It is a timing problem, and timing problems respond to timing.
