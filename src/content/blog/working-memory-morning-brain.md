It is 7:12 on a Thursday, and your bank wants a six-digit code. It arrives as a banner: 481 726. You switch back to the login page, and by the time the cursor is blinking in the box you have the 4, the 8 and a vague feeling about a 7. You go back and read it again. This time you mutter it under your breath all the way to the box, like a password at a speakeasy.

Meanwhile you are standing over the French press, scoop in hand, genuinely unsure whether that was the second scoop or the third.

Your memory is not failing. What is struggling is the workspace that holds a few things in mind for a few seconds while you use them. It has a name, a size, and a slow start in the morning.

**The short answer:** working memory is the mental workspace that holds information for a few seconds while you use it, like the code you are typing or the digit you carry in a sum. It is small, about three to five chunks for young adults rather than the famous seven, and its hardest jobs are among the last to recover after you wake. Memory games mostly make you better at memory games; sleep, a short runway after waking and writing things down do more for it.

## What is working memory?

Working memory holds information temporarily and works on it at the same time. Alan Baddeley defined it as the temporary storage and manipulation of the information needed for understanding language, learning and reasoning ([Baddeley, *Science*, 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1736359/)). Repeating a code back is storage; checking it against the screen while you type is working memory.

The model most psychologists start from was proposed by Baddeley and Hitch in 1974, with three parts:

- **The phonological loop** stores and rehearses speech-based information: the voice muttering 481 726.
- **The visuospatial sketchpad** handles images and positions, like which card was the queen.
- **The central executive** is an attention-controlling system that decides what gets held and what gets dropped.

In 2000 Baddeley added a fourth, the episodic buffer, which binds information from the other parts and from long-term memory into a single episode ([Baddeley, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11058819/)).

## How much can working memory hold?

Less than you have been told. Miller's 1956 paper made "the magical number seven" famous, but numbers that big come from tests that let you rehearse and group. Strip those tricks out and Nelson Cowan's review puts the underlying limit at 3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults ([Cowan, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)). Rehearsal stretches it a little: the inner voice can keep about two seconds of speech on a loop.

The useful idea is the chunk: whatever your memory treats as one unit. Make the units bigger and more fits. Cowan's example: to remember bread, milk and pepper, picture bread floating in peppery milk. It is also why 481 726 is easier as two numbers than as six digits.

## Working memory examples

Cowan's classics are holding the start of a sentence until its end arrives, carrying a digit from the ones column to the tens, and planning the best order for a run of errands. Mornings add their own:

- Holding a verification code between one app and another.
- Keeping count of coffee scoops.
- Following spoken directions without writing them down.
- Walking into the kitchen and still knowing why.

Each fails the same way: something else takes the slot.

## How does sleep affect working memory?

Less dramatically than you might expect. Sleep loss goes after attention first. Lim and Dinges pooled 147 cognitive tests from 70 articles on people kept awake for 24 to 48 hours: lapses of simple attention took the largest hit, and working memory a moderate one ([*Psychological Bulletin*, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3290659/)).

One short night is a weaker case. A 2024 meta-analysis of 44 studies of a single night cut to two to six hours found more sleepiness and worse sustained attention, but no significant effect on working memory ([Wüst et al., *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/)). Stack the nights up and it starts to show, unevenly: after five nights of five hours in bed, volunteers got worse at a spatial working memory task but not at a verbal one ([Hennecke et al., *Journal of Sleep Research*, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33169493/)). A University of Surrey study found the same ranking: sleepiness and sustained attention first, demanding working memory tasks much less ([Lo et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3454374/)).

So after a short night, what feels like forgetting is probably attention failing first: as Cowan notes, attention decides what gets into the workspace at all. If it blinks while the code is on screen, the code never gets in.

The minutes after waking are a more specific story. When Balkin and colleagues imaged people waking from stage 2 (light) sleep, blood flow returned first to the brainstem and thalamus, then rose over the next 15 minutes mainly in the front of the cortex, in regions the authors linked to grogginess wearing off ([*Brain*, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12244087/)). Groeger and colleagues tested working memory about 5 and 25 minutes after afternoon naps of up to 90 minutes. Nappers matched people who had stayed awake on the easy version of the task but fell behind on the hard one, tracking items three steps back, and the gap was gone after about 30 minutes of mental work ([*Behavioral Neuroscience*, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21463024/)).

That is a nap study of 32 people, not your alarm, so treat it as a pattern rather than a stopwatch: simple recall comes back quickly, and juggling comes back last. The reaction-time side of the same window is in [our piece on your reaction time at 7 AM](/blog/morning-reaction-time-cognition).

## Can you improve working memory with brain training?

You can get better at a working memory task. Whether that improves your working memory is a separate question, and the best evidence says mostly no.

In one of the largest tests, 11,430 people trained several times a week for six weeks on reasoning, memory, planning, visuospatial and attention tasks. They improved on every task they practised and showed no transfer to untrained tasks, even closely related ones ([Owen et al., *Nature*, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884087/)). A meta-analysis of 87 publications found gains on other working memory tests right after training, but no convincing gain in reading, arithmetic or intelligence tests ([Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033/)). A review of the studies that brain-training companies themselves cited found extensive evidence for the trained tasks and little for everyday cognitive performance ([Simons et al., *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697851/)). And in 2016 the company behind Lumosity agreed to pay $2 million to settle Federal Trade Commission charges over ads claiming the games helped people perform better at work and in school ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program)).

None of that makes puzzles pointless. They are a fine way to spend a coffee, and [a dozen quick math puzzles](/blog/math-puzzles-for-adults) will happily fill one. Just expect to get better at puzzles.

## How to improve working memory day to day

If you cannot make the workspace bigger, you can stop wasting it.

### 1. Sleep enough, for the attention that feeds it

The CDC recommends 7 or more hours a night for adults aged 18 to 60 ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)). A short night costs attention before it costs working memory, and attention is the doorkeeper. If you know when you have to get up, the [sleep calculator](/tools/sleep-calculator) works back to a bedtime with room for seven hours or more.

### 2. Give the first half hour jobs that fail safely

The nap data suggest the hard version of working memory lags for roughly half an hour after waking. Save the juggling for later: the transfer with the account number, the email with three figures in it. Give the first half hour the shower, the coffee and the walk, where a lapse costs nothing.

### 3. Write it down before you need to remember it

Psychologists call this cognitive offloading: using a physical action, like setting a phone reminder, to cut what a task demands of your head ([Risko and Gilbert, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/)). Tonight, write tomorrow's first three jobs on paper and leave it by the kettle. A note does not get groggy.

### 4. Chunk it and say it

Read 481 726 as two numbers, not six digits, and say them under your breath on the way to the box. The phonological loop rehearses speech, about two seconds of it, so give it something short. For a list, make one image, the way Cowan's bread floats in peppery milk.

### 5. Protect the juggling from interruptions

In a lab task where people had to keep their place in a sequence of steps, interruptions averaging 2.8 seconds doubled the rate of sequence errors ([Altmann, Trafton and Hambrick, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23294345/)). In another study, a phone notification alone, unanswered, disrupted an attention-demanding task about as much as actual calling or texting had in earlier research ([Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/)). For the jobs in fix 2, mute notifications and finish the step you are on before you look up.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between working memory and short-term memory?

Short-term memory holds information briefly; working memory holds it and does something with it. Repeating a phone number back is the first; adding up its digits is the second. The line is blurry, because the working memory model grew out of the older idea of a single short-term store, so the terms often get used interchangeably.

### Do memory games improve working memory?

They improve your performance on the games. In the largest training studies and meta-analyses, people got better at what they practised and, right after training, at similar memory tests, without convincing gains in reading, arithmetic, intelligence tests or everyday thinking. If you enjoy them, play them for that.

### Is poor working memory a sign of ADHD?

It can be part of it, but a slip is not a diagnosis. Across 38 studies, adults with ADHD scored moderately lower than comparison groups on verbal and visuospatial working memory tasks ([Alderson et al., *Neuropsychology*, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/)). Group averages say little about one person, and the same slips show up after lost sleep and just after waking. If trouble holding things in mind has followed you for years and gets in the way at work or at home, raise it with a clinician. If mornings are where it bites, see our piece on [time blindness](/blog/time-blindness-adhd-mornings).

### Is it normal to be forgetful in the morning?

A little, yes. The NHS notes that most people forget things from time to time, and lists stress, anxiety, depression and sleep problems among the common, treatable causes of memory loss ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/memory-loss-amnesia/)). See a doctor if memory problems are affecting your day-to-day life, or if an older relative is becoming increasingly forgetful. Sudden confusion, or sudden trouble speaking or understanding speech, can be a sign of stroke: call 911 right away ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/signs-symptoms/index.html)).

## Tomorrow at 7:12

The code will arrive again tomorrow, and the workspace will be exactly as small. That is not a flaw; it is the size the system comes in. What you can change is what you ask of it before it is ready: two numbers, said on the way to the box, and the real juggling saved for after the coffee. Small workspace, sensible jobs.
